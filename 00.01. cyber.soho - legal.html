3. Purpose of the Materials — Education Only

3.1 The Materials are created and made available for one purpose: to teach cybersecurity for defensive ends — the observation, monitoring, protection, assessment, testing, diagnosis, remediation and improvement of computer systems and networks that the User is authorized to work on.

3.1.1 Several Courses necessarily teach offensive technique — scanning, exploitation, password attacks, the handling of live malicious code — because no one can defend a system without understanding how it is attacked, and because testing a system's defences is itself a defensive discipline. Teaching a technique for a defensive purpose does not make that technique lawful outside an Authorized Lab Environment or outside the scope of a written authorization. The purpose protects nobody; the authorization does.

6. Acceptable Use Policy

6.1 The rule that governs everything else: authorization

6.1.1 You may apply the tools, techniques and knowledge in the Materials only to computer systems, networks, devices, accounts, communications and data that (a) you own, or (b) you have been expressly authorized, in writing, by the owner to work on, for the specific purpose and during the specific period concerned.

6.1.2 Neither the Author, nor Cyber.SoHo, nor the College can authorize you to access or test a system that they do not own. Authorization to monitor, scan, capture traffic on or otherwise interact with a system can only be granted by the owner of that system or the owner's legal representative. "I am a cybersecurity student", "it was for a course", "I did not cause any damage" and "I only wanted to see if it was possible" are not defences under the law.

6.1.3 If you are not certain that you have authorization, you do not have it. Stop and ask before acting.

6.1.4 Section 7 adds rules for particular Courses — monitoring and capture, threat information and samples, defensive configuration changes, audit work, vulnerability scanning, intrusion testing, malware handling, and work involving real organizations. Read the subsections that apply to your Course before its first laboratory. They add to this section; none of them relaxes it.

6.2 Permitted activities

Within the limits of this Notice and Applicable Law, you may:

  • build and use an Authorized Lab Environment on your own equipment, or on equipment or cloud resources that you rent or are permitted to use for that purpose, in compliance with the provider's terms;
  • install, configure and run the tools a Course designates — monitoring tools, packet analyzers, log collectors, intrusion detection and prevention systems, vulnerability scanners, exploitation frameworks, forensic and malware-analysis tooling — inside your Authorized Lab Environment and subject to section 7;

6.3 Prohibited activities

You must not, in connection with the Materials or otherwise:

  • access, or attempt to access, any computer system, network, device, account, service or data without authorization, or beyond the scope of your authorization;
  • scan, enumerate, probe, fingerprint, map or test any network or system that is not part of your Authorized Lab Environment — this includes the College's networks and systems, your Internet service provider's network, your employer's network, public Wi-Fi networks, and any website, server or cloud service belonging to a third party;
  • capture, sniff, intercept, record, decrypt or analyze network traffic or communications on any network you do not own or control, or on which other people's communications may travel, including shared home networks, residences, workplaces, campus networks and public hotspots;

  • bypass, disable or circumvent any security control, authentication mechanism, technological protection measure, licence restriction or access control;
  • use credentials, keys, tokens, certificates or configuration data that were provided for a lab exercise on any other system, or share them with anyone;
  • use real personal data, real credentials or real production data of any organization in any lab, exercise, submission, screenshot or recording;

6.4 Laboratory environment rules

  • Isolate. Run all laboratory work in virtual machines or on a dedicated lab network. Use host-only or internal networking for lab traffic. Do not bridge a lab network to the College's network, a corporate network or the Internet unless the exercise instructions expressly require it and explain how to do so safely.
  • Snapshot and back up. Take a snapshot of every virtual machine before an exercise, and back up any personal data on the host computer. Monitoring tools, packet capture and log collection can consume large amounts of disk, memory and processor time, can fill file systems and can crash systems.
  • Use only designated targets. Send traffic only to the lab systems, addresses and ranges designated for the exercise. Never use an address range, host name or domain name you do not control as a "test" target.
  • College equipment and networks. Laboratory activities on College equipment or networks are permitted only as expressly configured and supervised by the Author within the scheduled session, and in accordance with the College's rules on the use of its information technology resources.
  • Clean up. When an exercise is finished, stop the tools, delete captured data that you do not need for your submission, and restore your snapshot if required.
  • Keep it fictional. Use the fictional organization, users, systems and data provided for the Course exercise. Do not substitute a real organization, a real employer or real people.
  • Rented and cloud equipment. If you use rented, borrowed or cloud resources, you alone are responsible for the provider's terms, for any charges you incur (including unexpected charges caused by runaway tools), and for any suspension, termination or penalty imposed by the provider.

6.5 Tools with dual-use potential

Many tools studied in the Materials — including packet analyzers, port scanners, network mappers, vulnerability scanners, exploitation frameworks, password-attack tools, intrusion detection systems, log analysis platforms, endpoint monitoring agents and malware-analysis sandboxes — are lawful and widely used by professionals, yet the same tools can be used to commit offences. Possessing a tool is not the problem; using it against a system you are not authorized to touch is. Treat every such tool as you would treat a locksmith's tools: legitimate in your own house, an offence in someone else's.

6.6 Software licences

You are responsible for obtaining, and complying with, valid licences for all software you use in connection with the Materials, including operating systems, virtualization software, monitoring tools and their components. Use only official distribution sources. Do not use cracked, pirated, "patched" or unlicensed software, and do not circumvent trial or evaluation restrictions. Open-source software is subject to its own licence terms, which you must respect.

6.7 Data and privacy in laboratories

Monitoring inevitably collects data, and data about a network is often data about people. In all laboratory work you must: use only fictional or synthetic data; never capture or retain the traffic, logs, files or communications of any real person without consent; never post screenshots, captures or logs that contain real personal information, real credentials or the real internal addresses of any organization; and delete captured data when it is no longer needed. Personal information you encounter by accident must be treated as confidential and must not be copied, used or disclosed.

6.8 Accidental discovery and responsible disclosure

If, during any activity, you accidentally discover a vulnerability, a misconfiguration, exposed data or evidence of a security incident on a system that is not part of your Authorized Lab Environment:

  1. stop immediately — do not explore further, do not test whether it can be exploited, and do not copy or download anything;
  2. do not disclose it publicly, on social media or to other students;
  3. document only what you already observed (time, what you saw, how you came across it);
  4. report it promptly to the system owner or to the person responsible for the security of that system (for College systems, the College's IT services) and, for course-related situations, inform the Author.

Responsible disclosure protects you as much as it protects the system's owner.

7. Rules That Change from Course to Course

7.1 The escalation principle

7.1.1 The Program is deliberately built as a ladder. In the early Courses you observe a network and learn to recognize what is wrong with it. Later you scan systems for weaknesses, then test those weaknesses, then handle live malicious code and real incident data. Each rung is more intrusive than the last, and the consequences of a mistake grow with it.

7.1.2 The authorization rule in section 6.1 never changes and is never relaxed by anything in this section. What changes from Course to Course is how much written authorization you need before you act, and how much damage you can do if you get it wrong. Every subsection below adds to section 6; none of them subtracts from it.

7.1.3 A technique you are taught in a later Course is not retroactively permitted in an earlier one, and a technique taught in any Course is never permitted outside an Authorized Lab Environment or outside the scope of a written authorization. Finishing a Course does not extend your permissions; it extends your responsibility.

7.1.4 Where a Course laboratory guide, scope document or the Instructor's written instruction imposes a stricter rule than this section, the stricter rule applies.

7.2 Monitoring, capture and surveillance data

Applies chiefly to 420-1C1-DW Preventative Monitoring and 420-2C1-DW Cyber Defense.

  • Capture, sniff or log traffic only on interfaces of systems you own or that are Designated Targets, and only for the duration of the exercise.
  • Treat every capture, log file, baseline and alert record as personal information about the people who used those systems: store it in the lab, keep the minimum, and delete it when the exercise is marked.
  • Never install a monitoring agent, sensor, collector or logging tool on a device belonging to another person, an employer or the College, regardless of who asks you to.
  • Never enable promiscuous or monitor mode on a network you share with other people. Capturing on a shared network records other people's communications and may constitute interception under section 184 of the Criminal Code.
  • Monitoring another individual — a partner, relative, roommate, classmate or colleague — is never a Course activity, never a defence, and may be a criminal offence as well as an invasion of privacy.

7.3 Threat information, indicators and samples

Applies chiefly to 420-1C2-DW Threats and Risks and 420-4C1-DW Incident Management.

  • Study threat reports, indicators of compromise and attack patterns as information. Do not act on them against any live infrastructure.
  • Do not visit, resolve, probe or interact with live malicious infrastructure — command-and-control hosts, phishing pages, malware distribution sites — from a personal device, a College device, a College network or a home network. If an exercise requires examining such a resource, use only the environment the Instructor designates, in the manner the Instructor specifies.
  • Do not contact, engage, provoke, negotiate with, "investigate" or attempt to infiltrate any threat actor, criminal forum, marketplace, ransomware portal or messaging channel. This is not research; it is exposure — legal, technical and personal.
  • Do not obtain, download, purchase, possess, search or redistribute breach data, stolen credential dumps or leaked databases, including "to check whether my own data is in it". Possession of that data creates its own liability.
  • Do not download malware samples from public repositories on your own initiative. Use only samples the Instructor provides, in the environment the Instructor designates, subject to section 7.8.
  • Do not publish or repeat an attribution — naming a person, group, company or country as responsible for an attack — as though it were established fact. Course work is not evidence and you are not in a position to make that accusation.

7.4 Defensive tooling and configuration changes

Applies chiefly to 420-2C1-DW Cyber Defense and 420-4C2-DW Implementation and Management of Corporate Cybersecurity Measures.

  • A defensive change is still a change. Firewall rules, access control lists, hardening baselines, group policies, detection rules, quarantine actions and blocking responses can cut off legitimate users, break production services and cause outages.
  • Never apply a configuration, script, rule set or hardening guide from a Course to a live system without (a) the written approval of whoever owns that system, (b) a change window agreed with them, and (c) a tested rollback.
  • Test every change in the Authorized Lab Environment first, and keep the before-and-after state.
  • A blocking or containment action that disrupts a third party's lawful use of a system can amount to mischief in relation to computer data under section 430(1.1) of the Criminal Code, whatever your intention was.
  • Automated response and "active defence" measures that reach out to, disable or retaliate against another system are prohibited outright, in every Course, without exception.

7.5 Audit, compliance and confidential findings

Applies chiefly to 420-2C2-DW Compliance Assessments and 420-4C2-DW Implementation and Management of Corporate Cybersecurity Measures.

  • Standards and framework documents are licensed works. Some — including the ISO/IEC 27000 family and PCI DSS materials — may not be copied or redistributed. Obtain them through the College library, the Bibliothèque et Archives nationales du Québec or the publisher, and do not share your copy.
  • Do not use a real organization's policies, procedures, audit evidence, contracts, risk registers, gap analyses, network documentation or assessment reports in Course work without the written permission of that organization, and never those of an employer or client without their express written approval.
  • Every finding, weakness or gap you learn about a real organization — including through a placement, an internship or a family business — is confidential, indefinitely.
  • Course work is not an audit, an assessment, a certification or an attestation, and you must not describe it as one, sign anything as an assessor, or allow an organization to rely on it.
  • Where an assessment would process personal information, the privacy rules in section 6.7 and the legislation in section 8.3 apply in full, including Québec's Law 25.

7.6 Vulnerability scanning

Applies chiefly to 420-3C1-DW Managing Vulnerabilities.

  • A scan is an intrusive act, not an observation. Port scans, service enumeration, vulnerability checks and credentialed assessments write to logs, trigger alerts, consume resources and can crash fragile services. From the receiving end, a scan is indistinguishable from the first stage of an attack, and is routinely reported as one.
  • Scan only Designated Targets, in the designated address range, during the exercise. There is no such thing as a harmless "quick ping sweep" of a campus, residence, workplace or public network.
  • Never scan an address range you do not control because it "looked empty", because it answered, or because it was in the same subnet as a Designated Target.
  • Credentialed scans require the authorization of the owner of those credentials and of the target system, in writing.
  • If a lab target is hosted with a cloud or hosting provider, read that provider's policy on security testing before you scan it; most require notice or prohibit it entirely, and breaching it can terminate your account and your instructor's.
  • Never publish, post or submit raw scan output that identifies a real host, service, version or organization.

7.7 Intrusion testing and exploitation

Applies chiefly to 420-3C2-DW Intrusion Test.

7.7.1 This is the highest-risk Course in the Program. Exploiting a vulnerability without authorization is the conduct that sections 342.1 and 430(1.1) of the Criminal Code were written for, and a successful exploit leaves proof of what you did on the target.

7.7.2 Nothing is in scope until it is written down. Before any test, there must be a scope document — for laboratory work, the laboratory guide is that document — which names: the systems in scope; the systems expressly excluded; the techniques permitted and prohibited; the dates and hours of the test window; and who to contact if something breaks. If it is not named there, it is out of scope. A target that is reachable is not a target that is authorized.

7.7.3 The following are prohibited in every circumstance connected to the Program:

  • testing, exploiting or "checking" any system, site, application or service outside the scope document, including third-party websites, cloud services, bug-bounty targets and capture-the-flag platforms, under cover of the Course;

7.8 Incident response, forensics and malware handling

Applies chiefly to 420-4C1-DW Incident Management.

  • Execute, detonate or "run to see what it does" a malicious sample only inside the isolated analysis environment designated for that exercise: snapshotted, with networking disabled or host-only, with shared folders, shared clipboard and drag-and-drop turned off, and with no access to your personal data.
  • Never execute a sample on a host operating system, a personal device, a phone, College equipment outside the designated environment, or any employer-owned device.
  • Never e-mail, message, upload, sync, print or otherwise move a sample out of the analysis environment, and never share one with a classmate, a forum or a social media contact. Distributing malicious code is an offence in its own right.
  • Do not upload samples or artefacts containing confidential or personal data to public multi-engine scanning services: submissions to those services become available to their subscribers.
  • Restore the snapshot when the exercise ends, and confirm that the sample is gone.
  • Handle evidence as though it mattered: preserve originals, work on copies, record hashes, record times, and record what you did. Then remember that a Course exercise is not admissible forensic work, that you are not a forensic examiner, and that you must never present Course work as an expert opinion.
  • If you encounter a real incident at your workplace, follow your employer's incident response plan and chain of command. A Course exercise is not a mandate to investigate, contain or remediate anything at work.

8. Legal Warning — Applicable Laws and Penalties

8.1 The following is a non-exhaustive summary provided for information only. It is not legal advice. Statutes are amended from time to time; consult the current consolidated text of each statute (federal law: https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca; Québec law: https://www.legisquebec.gouv.qc.ca) and, where necessary, a lawyer.

8.2 Criminal Code of Canada (R.S.C. 1985, c. C-46)

The Criminal Code applies throughout Canada. The offences most directly relevant to the subject matter of the Materials include:

  • Unauthorized use of a computer (s. 342.1). Fraudulently and without colour of right obtaining any computer service, intercepting any function of a computer system, using a computer system with intent to commit such an offence or to commit mischief in relation to computer data, or using, possessing or trafficking in a computer password for those purposes. Maximum penalty: ten years of imprisonment. Merely connecting to a network or using a service that you are not entitled to use can fall under this section.
  • Possession of a device to obtain unauthorized use of a computer system or to commit mischief (s. 342.2). Making, possessing, distributing or making available a device — which includes software — designed or adapted primarily to commit an offence under s. 342.1 or s. 430, knowing that it has been or is intended to be used for that purpose. Maximum penalty: two years of imprisonment, plus forfeiture of the device.
  • Mischief in relation to computer data (s. 430(1.1)). Wilfully destroying or altering data, rendering data meaningless, useless or ineffective, or obstructing, interrupting or interfering with the lawful use of data or with a person's lawful use of or access to data. Maximum penalty: ten years of imprisonment. Denial-of-service activity typically falls under this section.
  • Interception of private communications (s. 184). Knowingly intercepting a private communication by means of any device. Maximum penalty: five years of imprisonment. Capturing other people's network traffic can constitute interception.
  • Identity theft and identity fraud (ss. 402.2 and 403). Obtaining or possessing another person's identity information with intent to commit an offence, or fraudulently personating another person. Maximum penalties: five and ten years of imprisonment respectively.
  • Fraud (s. 380), extortion (s. 346), criminal harassment (s. 264), uttering threats (s. 264.1), false information and harassing communications (s. 372) and related offences may also apply, depending on the conduct.
  • A person who counsels, aids or abets another person to commit an offence, or who is otherwise a party to it, is also criminally liable (ss. 21 to 23).

A criminal conviction may result in imprisonment, fines, a criminal record, forfeiture of equipment, probation conditions restricting computer use, and permanent exclusion from careers that require a security clearance or a background check — the very careers this program prepares you for.

8.3 Privacy and data protection

  • Civil Code of Québec (arts. 3 and 35 to 41): every person has a right to the respect of their private life. Intercepting or using a person's private communications, keeping a person's private life under observation by any means, or using a person's name, image, voice or correspondence without consent may constitute an invasion of privacy (art. 36).
  • Charter of Human Rights and Freedoms (CQLR c. C-12), s. 5: right to respect for one's private life.
  • Act respecting the protection of personal information in the private sector (CQLR c. P-39.1), as amended by "Law 25" (S.Q. 2021, c. 25): governs the collection, use, communication and retention of personal information by enterprises. It provides for administrative monetary penalties of up to $10,000,000 or 2% of worldwide turnover, and penal fines of up to $25,000,000 or 4% of worldwide turnover, for enterprises, and for substantial fines for individuals.
  • Act respecting Access to documents held by public bodies and the Protection of personal information (CQLR c. A-2.1): applies to personal information held by public bodies, including the College.
  • Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act (S.C. 2000, c. 5, "PIPEDA"): federal private-sector privacy law.
  • Outside Canada, the General Data Protection Regulation (EU) 2016/679 and similar laws may apply if you process personal data of persons located abroad.

8.4 Other Canadian legislation

  • Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation (S.C. 2010, c. 23, "CASL"): prohibits installing a computer program on another person's computer system without express consent (s. 8) and altering transmission data in electronic messages without consent (s. 7), in addition to regulating commercial electronic messages (s. 6). Deploying a monitoring agent on a device you do not own can violate CASL as well as the Criminal Code.
  • Copyright Act (R.S.C. 1985, c. C-42): prohibits, among other things, the circumvention of technological protection measures (s. 41.1) and the unauthorized reproduction and distribution of protected works, including software and the Materials.
  • The terms of service of Internet service providers, telecommunications carriers and cloud providers restrict interference with, and unauthorized testing of, their networks and services, and are enforceable against you contractually.

8.5 Civil liability

Independently of any criminal or penal proceeding, a person who causes injury to another through their fault is bound to repair it (Civil Code of Québec, art. 1457). Unauthorized access, interception, data destruction, service disruption, invasion of privacy and defamation can all give rise to civil actions for damages, including business losses, incident response costs and moral injury. Comparable liability exists in every other jurisdiction.

8.6 Other jurisdictions

The Internet has no borders. If a system, service, cloud region, data subject or victim is located outside Canada, the laws of that place may apply to you in addition to Canadian law, and extradition is possible for serious offences. Examples include the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (18 U.S.C. § 1030) and the Wiretap Act (18 U.S.C. § 2511) in the United States, the Computer Misuse Act 1990 in the United Kingdom, and Directive 2013/40/EU on attacks against information systems as implemented by each member state of the European Union.

  5. Verify the checksum (SHA-256) or digital signature published by the publisher against the file you downloaded.
  6. Scan the file: https://www.virustotal.com/gui/home/upload (do not upload files that contain confidential information).
  7. Open unknown files only inside a virtual machine that has a snapshot and no access to your personal data.
  8. If in doubt, do not click. Ask the Author or the College's IT services.

Annex D — Written Authorization to Monitor, Scan or Test a System (template)

A Student who wishes to practise on a network that belongs to someone else — a relative, an employer, a small business, a community organization — must obtain written authorization from the owner before installing any tool, capturing any traffic, running any scan or attempting any test. Verbal permission is not enough, and permission from someone who does not own the systems is worth nothing. The following wording may be used.

I, ____ (name, title), confirm that I am the owner, or a representative entitled to bind the owner, of the following systems and network: ___ (description, address ranges, host names, accounts). I authorize _____ (student name, student number) to carry out the following activities on those systems, for educational purposes only:

☐ install and operate monitoring tools ☐ capture and analyze network traffic and logs ☐ review configurations and documentation ☐ run vulnerability scans ☐ attempt authorized intrusion testing (specify permitted techniques: ________)

Systems and addresses expressly excluded from this authorization: ________

Period: from __ (date, time) to _ (date, time). Contact if something breaks: _____ (name, telephone).

I understand that these activities may affect the availability and performance of these systems and may record information about the people who use them. I confirm that I have the authority to give this authorization, that the persons who use these systems have been informed and have consented as required by law, and that no third party's systems or data are covered by it. Signature: ____ Date: ____

The Student keeps the signed original for the duration of the Program and gives a copy to the Instructor before starting. This template is a practical aid, not legal advice; for work of any significance, the owner should take their own advice.

Annex E — Course Quick Reference

A summary only. The full rule is in the section named beside each Course; where this Annex and the body of the Notice differ, the body governs.

420-1C1-DW — Preventative Monitoring (75 h, Term 1)

  • Added rules: section 7.2 — monitoring, capture and surveillance data.
  • The line: capture on Designated Targets inside the lab, never on a shared network.
  • Most likely way to get it wrong: running a capture on home or campus Wi-Fi "to have real traffic to analyze".

420-1C2-DW — Threats and Risks (75 h, Term 1)

  • Added rules: section 7.3 — threat information, indicators and samples.
  • The line: study threat information; never interact with live malicious infrastructure or its operators.
  • Most likely way to get it wrong: opening a phishing or command-and-control URL from a personal machine to "see what it does", or downloading a breach dump to check a personal address.

420-2C1-DW — Cyber Defense (90 h, Term 1)

  • Added rules: sections 7.2 and 7.4 — capture data, and defensive tooling and configuration changes.
  • The line: every defensive change is a change; it needs an owner's written approval, a window and a rollback.
  • Most likely way to get it wrong: applying a hardening baseline or firewall rule to a live home, work or College system and cutting off its users.

420-2C2-DW — Compliance Assessments (45 h, Term 2)

  • Added rules: section 7.5 — audit, compliance and confidential findings.
  • The line: no real organization's documents or evidence without written permission; every finding is confidential; your work is not an audit.
  • Most likely way to get it wrong: using an employer's real policies, contracts or gap analysis as the case study, or redistributing a licensed standard.

420-3C1-DW — Managing Vulnerabilities (60 h, Term 2)

  • Added rules: section 7.6 — vulnerability scanning.
  • The line: scan only the designated range; a scan is an intrusive act, and it is logged as an attack.
  • Most likely way to get it wrong: a "quick sweep" of the local subnet, the residence network or a cloud-hosted target whose provider prohibits testing.

420-3C2-DW — Intrusion Test (90 h, Term 2)

  • Added rules: section 7.7 — intrusion testing and exploitation. The strictest rules in the Program.
  • The line: nothing is in scope until a written scope document names it; leave nothing behind; stop and report the instant you are outside scope.
  • Most likely way to get it wrong: treating a reachable host, a bug-bounty target or a public CTF as though the Course authorized it.

420-4C1-DW — Incident Management (75 h, Term 2)

  • Added rules: sections 7.3 and 7.8 — samples, incident response, forensics and malware handling.
  • The line: samples run only in the designated isolated environment, and never leave it; evidence is preserved, not altered; you are not a forensic examiner.
  • Most likely way to get it wrong: detonating a sample on the host operating system, or sending one to a classmate or a public service.

420-4C2-DW — Implementation and Management of Corporate Cybersecurity Measures (60 h, Term 3)

  • Added rules: sections 7.4, 7.5 and 7.9 — configuration changes, confidential findings, and work involving a real organization.
  • The line: written authorization before touching anything real, the employer's rules take precedence, and nothing real enters a submission.
  • Most likely way to get it wrong: implementing a Course design on a real employer's or family business's network without written authorization from the person actually entitled to give it.
