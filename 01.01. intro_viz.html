The competency statement: EZ01Slides 6–7

A Québec AEC (Attestation of College Studies, in French Attestation d'études collégiales) programme is built from competencies. A competency is a formally worded description of something a graduate must be able to do, published by the Ministère de l'Enseignement supérieur and reproduced without alteration in every course outline. This course addresses one competency, coded EZ01, in its entirety:

Monitor a computer network from a cybersecurity perspective.

Three structural parts sit underneath that one sentence, and you should learn their names because they reappear in every course of the programme:

  • Elements of the competency — the five sub-abilities the competency decomposes into. For EZ01 these are: distinguishing cybersecurity concepts; analysing the topology of the network; installing and configuring surveillance tools; detecting and diagnosing anomalies; and optimising surveillance tools.
  • Performance criteria — the observable standards by which each element is judged. They are the wording I must use when I grade you. "Accurate interpretation of available data" is a performance criterion; "you did a good job" is not.
  • Achievement context — the conditions under which the competency must be demonstrated. For EZ01 the context is an entire documented network of mixed platforms, monitored on an ongoing basis, under supervision, in collaboration with other technicians and users, on the basis of policies, norms, standards and infrastructure documentation.

Read that context once more. It contains the two constraints that shape the whole course: you will work on a documented network, and you will work in collaboration. Neither an undocumented network nor a lone analyst appears anywhere in the standard, which is why the first laboratory asks you to produce documentation before it asks you to install anything.

The job description behind the competencySlide 10

EZ01 describes the entry-level work of a SOC (Security Operations Centre) analyst, sometimes titled monitoring technician or Tier 1 analyst. The dominant tool in that environment is a SIEM (Security Information and Event Management) platform, which collects log records from many systems into one searchable store and raises alerts from rules applied to them. You will meet SIEM concepts in Topic 3 and work with monitoring outputs in Topics 4 and 5.

The task. Atelier Nord-Ouest has asked for network monitoring. Before any software is chosen, you must write a monitoring scope register: a table that lists every asset to be monitored, states which element of competency EZ01 the work on that asset demonstrates, and names the evidence you will hand in. Why here. This is the professional equivalent of the course outline itself. The outline maps a competency to topics, classes and evaluations; the scope register maps a client mandate to assets, activities and deliverables. In practice a monitoring engagement fails in one of two ways. Either something important was never on the list, so nobody watched it, or the client believed something was on the list when it was not. Cumulative quizzesSlide 20

    
Each quiz covers all material presented up to its date, not only the material since the previous quiz. Quiz 4 on 15 October can therefore ask about the CIA triad taught on 17 September. This design is deliberate and is supported by a well-documented effect in cognitive psychology: retrieval practice, also called the testing effect, produces markedly better long-term retention than re-reading the same material. Spacing those retrievals across weeks, known as spaced repetition, strengthens the effect further. The quizzes are the schedule's way of forcing both.


    
Formative and summative assessmentSlide 18

    
Formative assessment produces feedback that informs your next attempt and does not determine your grade. Summative assessment measures achievement of the competency at the end and does determine it. In this course the final examination is the summative assessment element, and two rules follow from that status:

    

      
The two rules that decide whether you passSlide 18

      
Rule 1. You must obtain at least 60 % on the final examination. A final examination result below 60 % is a failure of the course, and the grade recorded for the whole course becomes that examination grade. A student with 88 % accumulated before the final and 52 % on the final does not receive 73 %; the recorded grade is 52 %.

      
Rule 2. You must also obtain at least 60 % overall. Passing the final examination does not by itself pass the course if the weighted total is below 60 %.

      
Both rules must hold. The program at the end of this lesson calculates them for you so that you can see, at any point in the term, what the final examination still has to deliver.

What preventative monitoring actually is

      
A precise definition, the pipeline it runs on, and the statistics underneath it · approx. 20 min

    

    
  

  


    
The definition, one clause at a timeSlide 25

    
Preventative monitoring is the continuous and systematic observation of a computer network and its systems, carried out in order to detect and report deviations from a known normal state early enough for action to be taken before damage occurs.

    
Every clause in that sentence is doing work. Take them separately.

    
    
      
  • Continuous. Observation runs at all times, not at intervals chosen by a technician. A weakness that is visible for forty minutes at three in the morning is either recorded or it is gone.
    • 
      
  • Systematic. What is observed, how often, and what counts as notable are all decided in advance and written down. Monitoring that depends on someone noticing something is not monitoring.
    • 
      
  • A known normal state. This is the baseline, also called the reference database: a recorded description of how the network behaves when nothing is wrong. Without it, no observation can be classified, because "unusual" has no meaning until "usual" has been measured.
    • 
      
  • Detect and report. Detection without reporting changes nothing. The competency's performance criteria include effective communication with stakeholders for this reason.
    • 
      
  • Before damage occurs. This is the word preventative. The observation itself prevents nothing; it buys the time in which someone else can act.
    • 
    


    

      
A distinction students are marked down for confusingSlide 25

      
Security controls are classified by when they act. A preventive control stops an event from happening: a firewall rule, an account lockout, a door lock. A detective control records that an event happened: a log, an alert, a camera. A corrective control restores the state afterwards: a restore from backup, a patch.

      
Monitoring is a detective control. It is called preventative monitoring because it is aimed at an early stage of an attack, where detection still leaves time to prevent the damage. The monitoring does not prevent; it enables prevention. Write it that way in your reports.

    


    
What is being protected: the CIA triadSlide 26

    
Monitoring exists to protect three properties of information, known together as the CIA triad (Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability). Each one fails in a different way and is detected by different evidence.

    

      
PropertyDefinitionTypical failure at Atelier Nord-OuestEvidence that reveals it

      
ConfidentialityInformation is disclosed only to those authorised to receive itA drawing folder is copied to an unknown external addressOutbound traffic volume and destination in flow records

      
IntegrityInformation is modified only in authorised, recorded waysDrawing files are silently altered or encryptedFile integrity checks, sudden mass file-write events

      
AvailabilityInformation and services are accessible when neededThe backup service stops and no one is toldService status checks, absence of expected log entries

    

    
Note the third row. The absence of an expected event is itself evidence. A backup server that has stopped writing its nightly success message is not quiet, it is failing, and a monitoring configuration that only alerts on events will never see it.


    
Security issue or performance issue?Slide 27

    
Element 1.4 of the competency asks for a clear distinction between performance and security issues, because the same symptom can come from either. A saturated internet link may mean a large legitimate file transfer, or a workstation exfiltrating data. A server at 100 % processor use may mean a scheduled report, or unauthorised cryptocurrency mining. The distinguishing question is not what changed but whether the change is explained. A performance issue has an owner, a cause and usually a change record. A security issue does not. This is why the baseline matters more than any single measurement.


    
The monitoring pipelineSlides 29–30

    
Every monitoring system, from a single script to a commercial platform, performs the same seven operations in the same order. Learn this sequence now; the remaining five topics of the course are its stages.

    
    
      
  1. Collect. Gather raw observations from sources.
    2. 
      
  2. Normalise. Convert records of different formats into one common structure, and put them on one clock. Systems whose clocks disagree produce evidence that cannot be correlated, which is why NTP (Network Time Protocol) synchronisation is a prerequisite for monitoring rather than a detail of it.
    3. 
      
  3. Baseline. Measure normal behaviour over a representative period and store it.
    4. 
      
  4. Detect. Compare incoming observations against the baseline and against known-bad patterns, and raise an alert when a rule fires.
    5. 
      
  5. Triage. Sort alerts by severity and credibility and discard the ones explained by ordinary activity.
    6. 
      
  6. Diagnose. Assemble the evidence for a surviving alert into a statement of what happened, to what, when, and with what effect.
    7. 
      
  7. Report and optimise. Communicate the finding to the people who can act, then adjust the rules so the next occurrence is caught faster and with fewer false alarms.
    8.

The statistics of a baselineSlide 32

    
A baseline is not a screenshot of a normal day. It is a statistical description. For a numeric measurement such as failed logons per hour you record many observations over a representative period and calculate two values:

    
    
      
  • The arithmetic mean, written μ, which is the sum of the observations divided by their number. It describes the centre of normal behaviour.
    • 
      
  • The standard deviation, written σ, which is the square root of the average squared distance of each observation from the mean. It describes how much normal behaviour varies.
    • 
    

    
A common detection threshold is μ + 3σ. For measurements that follow an approximately normal distribution, roughly 99.7 % of normal observations fall within three standard deviations of the mean, so a value above that line is unusual enough to be worth a human's attention while still being rare enough not to flood the queue. The choice of three is a working convention and nothing more: raise it and you miss real events, lower it and you drown in false alarms. Topic 6 is largely about choosing that number well.

    
Two qualifications matter in practice. First, the baseline must cover a representative period. Network activity has daily and weekly cycles, so a baseline built from a Tuesday afternoon will alarm every Monday morning. Second, the baseline must be rebuilt when the network legitimately changes. Section 5.1 of the competency, "comprehensive update of reference databases", exists because a stale baseline is worse than none: it produces confident, wrong answers.

        Produces deliverable D4, the Lab Environment Conformity Report attached to Lab 1, Lab 2 and Lab 3
      

      

        
The task. Before starting any exercise you must be able to demonstrate, in writing, that your environment satisfies the three conditions of an Authorized Lab Environment. You will run a short script that inspects each virtual machine's network adapters, confirms that a snapshot exists, checks free disk space, and produces a dated report. Why here. Professionals do not assert that a system is isolated; they evidence it. In industry this is called a pre-change verification, and it is the same discipline that will later stop you from running a scan against an address range you did not intend.

        
The script deliberately finds a fault, because the version of this exercise where everything passes teaches nothing. One machine has a second adapter left in bridged mode from a download session. The script detects it, refuses to report conformity, and tells you which machine and which adapter to correct. That is the outcome you want from a check: a specific, actionable failure rather than a reassuring summary.

Step by step, in plain language

        
    
          
  1. #!/usr/bin/env bash on the first line is the shebang. It tells the operating system which interpreter should run the file, so the script can be executed directly rather than passed to bash by hand.
    2. 
          
  2. set -uo pipefail changes three default behaviours of the shell: using an undefined variable becomes an error, and a failure anywhere in a pipeline is reported rather than hidden. Scripts that make safety claims should not silently continue after a failed command.
    3. 
          
  3. VMS=( ... ) declares an array holding the three machine names. An array keeps related values in one variable so the loop below can walk through them without the names being repeated in the code.
    4. 
          
  4. FAIL=0 creates a counter. Every failed check increases it. At the end, a counter of zero means conformity and anything else means the report cannot be signed.
    5. 
          
  5. The first loop runs VBoxManage showvminfo for each machine and filters the output for the lines describing network adapters. VBoxManage is VirtualBox's command-line tool, and it reports exactly what the graphical settings screen shows, which is why the check is trustworthy.
    6. 
          
  6. Each adapter line is tested against the word Bridged. Finding it prints an alert, increases FAIL, and marks that machine red in the schematic. Watch the red path appear between AN-FS01 and the outside network when the script reaches it.
    7. 
          
  7. The second check asks for the snapshot list of each machine. An empty list is a failure, because a laboratory without a restore point violates section 6.4 and leaves you with no way back from a corrupted guest.
    8. 
          
  8. The third check reads free space with df and compares it with a threshold of 20 gigabytes. Capture files and log stores grow faster than students expect, and a disk that fills during a snapshot can corrupt the machine it was protecting.
    9. 
          
  9. The fourth check confirms that a signed copy of Annex A is present in the course folder. It is a file test, not a legal verification, and it exists to make the requirement visible rather than to prove compliance.
    10. 
          
  10. The final block prints the verdict and exits with a status code: 0 for conformity, 1 for a failure. An exit code lets the script be used by other tools later; in this course it simply tells you whether you may start the exercise.
    11. 
          
  11. Pause when the bridged adapter is found and read the remediation line. Fixing it is two steps in the graphical interface: Settings, Network, Adapter 2, change Attached to from Bridged Adapter to Internal Network, then re-run the check.
    12.

Check yourself — lesson 1.4
Four single-answer questions and two multiple-answer questions.


      

        
1. You need AN-BK01 to download package updates once, then take part in an isolated exercise. What is the correct sequence?

        
Single answer — select one of four

        
        
        
        
        

        
One mode gives outbound internet with no inbound exposure. One mode gives isolation. The exercise needs the second, and updating needs the first, so the answer involves changing modes and then verifying.

        
Correct: (c). NAT provides outbound access without exposing the guest to your real network, and switching to the internal network before the exercise restores segregation; the pre-flight check then confirms it, which is the step students skip. (a) leaves the machine on your real network indefinitely, which is precisely the prohibited configuration. (b) inverts both modes: host-only has no internet access, and bridged is never correct for an exercise. (d) is unnecessarily laborious and does not improve isolation over the internal network.

      


      

        
2. The pre-flight script exits with status 1 and reports a bridged adapter on AN-FS01. The laboratory is due in forty minutes. What is the correct action?

        
Single answer — select one of four

        
        
        
        
        

        
The check exists to be acted on. Ask which option leaves the machine on your real network while an exercise runs.

        
Correct: (d). The fault takes under a minute to correct and the check is fast, so there is no conflict between conformity and the deadline. (a) runs the exercise in a non-conforming environment and records the breach in writing. (b) removes the evidence rather than the fault. (c) relies on an assumption about what the guest will do, and a guest on a bridged adapter can receive and answer traffic from your real network whether or not you run a capture tool.

      


      

        
3. Why does the script check for the existence of a snapshot rather than only for adapter modes?

        
Single answer — select one of four

        
        
        
        
        

        
A snapshot is about recovery. Ask what it recovers you from, and which policy section names the requirement.

        
Correct: (b). The requirement is recoverability, and the practical risk is real: capture and log collection consume disk, memory and processor time, and can leave a guest in a state you cannot repair. (a) is false; a snapshot records state and does not restrict execution. (c) would breach section 17.2, which makes laboratory virtual machines confidential. (d) is false; a snapshot records a state, not a connection history.

      


      

        
4. A classmate offers you an Ubuntu Server image through a file-sharing link because the official download is slow. What do you do?

        
Single answer — select one of four

        
        
        
        
        

        
Section 14.2 and Annex C both address downloads. One of these options is the only one that can detect a single altered byte.

        
Correct: (a). Official source plus checksum verification is the procedure set out in section 14.2 and Annex C, and it is the only option here that detects tampering. (b) confuses licensing with integrity; open-source images are as easy to modify as any other file, which is why publishers sign and hash them. (c) inspects a system that has already been installed and possibly compromised, and antivirus detection is not a substitute for verifying the source. (d) is ineffective: file size is trivially preserved while contents are altered.

      


      

        
5. Which checks genuinely belong in a pre-flight conformity report for this course?

        
Multiple answers — select all that apply, from six

        
        
        
        
        
        
        

        
A conformity report evidences isolation, recoverability and stability. Two options here would themselves be breaches, and one tests the opposite of what the course requires.

        
Correct: (b), (d) and (e). Adapter modes evidence segregation, snapshots evidence recoverability, and free space evidences stability. (a) tests the opposite of the requirement; during an exercise the guests should not reach the internet at all. (c) breaches section 7.7.4 and elementary practice, since credentials are never written into a submitted document. (f) is not yours to obtain or distribute, and section 17.2 makes laboratory configurations confidential.

      


      

        
6. Which statements about the laboratory environment are correct?

        
Multiple answers — select all that apply, from six

        
        
        
        
        
        
        

        
Two options describe settings in the wrong place, and one confuses a user account with a network boundary. Section 10.3 answers the last one.

        
Correct: (a), (c) and (f). VirtualBox runs on top of your operating system, an internal network connects guests only, and section 10.3 places responsibility for backing up your own data on you. (b) is wrong: VT-x and AMD-V are enabled in the UEFI or BIOS setup screen of the physical machine, before any operating system loads. (d) is wrong, and the opposite is closer to the truth: after a snapshot, changes are written to a differencing file that grows as you work. (e) confuses user isolation with network isolation; the requirement concerns the network boundary and the absence of real personal data, and a shared family computer usually fails both.
