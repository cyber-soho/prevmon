A working definition

Slide 6 of the class presentation: What is cybersecurity?.

Cybersecurity is the protection of computers, networks, programs and digital information against six unwanted actions: unauthorized access, use, disclosure, disruption, modification and destruction. The protection is obtained by combining technologies, written procedures and the daily practices of people.

This wording follows the definitions published by two public bodies. The Canadian Centre for Cyber Security, the federal authority on the subject, describes cyber security as the protection of digital information and of the infrastructure that stores and transmits it. The glossary of the NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) in the United States lists the same six unwanted actions in its definition of information security.

You will meet three neighbouring expressions. They overlap, and many people use them as synonyms:

  • Information security protects information in every form, including paper files and spoken conversations.
  • IT (information technology) security protects the computer equipment and the software of an organization.
  • Cybersecurity protects what is digital and connected to a network. This is the scope of the present course.

The word unauthorized is the centre of the definition. The same action is normal when the right person does it and harmful when another person does it. A bookkeeper who opens a client's tax file is doing her work. A stranger who opens the same file is committing a breach. Security work therefore always begins with one question: who is allowed to do what, on which system?

The vocabulary of risk

Slides 7 and 8 of the class presentation: The vocabulary of risk: five words in order; Risk = likelihood x impact.

Five words describe almost every security situation. Learn them in this order, because each one depends on the one before.

  1. An asset is anything that has value for the organization and therefore needs protection. Information is an asset (the client files). Equipment is an asset (the file server). A service is an asset (the e-mail service). An account with its password is an asset.
  2. A threat is a possible cause of an unwanted event that can harm an asset. Some threats are intentional: a criminal group that sends fraudulent e-mails. Some are accidental: an employee who deletes a folder by mistake. Some are natural or technical: a power failure, a disk that stops working. The person or group behind an intentional threat is called a threat actor.
  3. A vulnerability is a weakness that a threat can use. Examples: software that no longer receives security updates, a password shared by the whole staff, a backup that nobody has ever tested, an employee who was never trained to recognize a fraudulent e-mail.
  4. A risk is the combination of two estimates: the likelihood that a threat uses a vulnerability, and the impact on the organization if it happens. In a simple qualitative method each estimate receives a number, for example from 1 to 5, and risk = likelihood × impact. A threat without a vulnerability, or a vulnerability on an asset without value, produces little risk.
  5. A control, also called a safeguard or a countermeasure, is a measure that reduces a risk. A control can be technical (a firewall, an automatic update, a second proof of identity at sign-in), administrative (a written policy, staff training, a procedure for departing employees) or physical (a locked cabinet for the server).

Here is one sentence that uses the five words about Grey Pine: the client files (asset) could be encrypted by a criminal group (threat) because the file server no longer receives updates (vulnerability); the likelihood is medium and the impact is very high (risk), so the office installs a supported version and tests its backups every month (controls).

No organization can bring every risk to zero. Money, time and staff are limited. The work consists of finding the highest risks and treating them first. This idea, priority, returns in every topic of this course, and it is the subject of the example at the end of this lesson.

Events, alerts and incidents, and the place of monitoring

Slides 9 and 10 of the class presentation: Event, alert, incident; Where preventative monitoring fits.

Three more words describe what a monitoring technician sees on the screen.

  • An event is any occurrence that can be observed in a system or on a network: a user signs in, a file is opened, the firewall refuses a connection, a disk reaches 80 percent. A small office produces thousands of events per day. Nearly all of them are normal.
  • An alert is a message produced by a tool when one or several events match a rule that somebody configured, for example five failed sign-ins on the same account in one minute.
  • An incident is an event, or a group of events, that harms or threatens the information or the systems of the organization, or that breaks one of its security rules. An alert is a question; after analysis, the technician decides whether it describes an incident or not.

Security activities fall into three groups. Prevention stops an unwanted action before it happens. Detection notices quickly that something abnormal is happening. Response limits the damage, corrects the cause and restores normal operation. Friday's class studies the three groups in detail.

Preventative monitoring, the title of this course, is the ongoing observation of a network in order to detect and report problems before they cause damage. It is a detection activity carried out early enough to have a preventive effect. A disk that is 90 percent full, a backup that failed last night, thirty failed sign-ins on the owner's account: none of these has caused damage yet, and each one will if nobody sees it.

Why organizations need it

Slides 11 and 12 of the class presentation: Why organizations need cybersecurity (1/2); Why organizations need cybersecurity (2/2): Canada, 2023.

There are five reasons. They apply to a bank and to an office of nine people.

1. The work depends on digital systems

At Grey Pine nobody can prepare a tax return when the file server or the accounting application does not answer. For Canadian businesses hit by an incident in 2021, Statistics Canada measured the consequence: about four in ten lost working time, 36 hours on average (The Daily, October 18, 2022). For an office that bills by the hour, 36 hours without systems is a direct loss of revenue.

2. Incidents are frequent and recovery costs are rising

The most recent national survey covers the year 2023. The table below gives the main results. Read the last two lines with attention: recovery spending doubled in two years, and the activity that businesses report most often is exactly the subject of this course.

3. The organization holds information that belongs to other people

Grey Pine keeps names, addresses, social insurance numbers, income and bank details of its clients. In Québec, the Act respecting the protection of personal information in the private sector, modernized by the statute known as Law 25, makes every enterprise responsible for the personal information it holds. Personal information is any information that concerns a natural person and allows that person to be identified. The Act names a confidentiality incident: an access, a use or a communication of personal information that the law does not authorize, or the loss of that information. When such an incident presents a risk of serious injury, the enterprise must notify the CAI (Commission d'accès à l'information du Québec) and the persons concerned, and it must record every incident in a register. The penalties reach 10 million dollars or 2 percent of worldwide turnover for administrative sanctions, and 25 million dollars or 4 percent for penal offences. At the federal level, the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada applies similar duties under the PIPEDA (Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act). This paragraph is general information, not legal advice (Legal Notice, section 16).

4. Clients, insurers and partners ask for proof

A client who hands over his tax documents expects them to stay private. An insurer that sells cyber risk insurance asks which controls are in place before it sets a price. A larger company that subcontracts work to a small one often adds security requirements to the contract. An organization that cannot show what it protects, and how, loses clients and contracts.

5. The threat is constant, and it does not choose by size

In its National Cyber Threat Assessment 2025-2026, the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security states that cybercrime remains a persistent and widespread threat to individuals and organizations in Canada, and it names ransomware as the first cybercrime threat to critical infrastructure. Criminal groups run automated searches for any reachable system that has a weakness. A small office is found by the same search as a large company, and it usually has fewer defences. For this reason the same Centre publishes Baseline cyber security controls for small and medium organizations. Its first steps are to decide which systems are in scope and which information has the most value. The example at the end of this lesson does exactly that.

Who is responsible, and your role

Slide 13 of the class presentation: Who is responsible, and your role.

Security is a shared duty with distinct roles. The owner or the management decides how much risk is acceptable, pays for the controls and answers for the result; in Québec the person with the highest authority is, by default, the person in charge of the protection of personal information. The administrator, an employee or an outside provider, installs and maintains the systems. Every employee applies the rules every day. The monitoring technician, the role you are training for, watches the tools, sorts the alerts, analyzes what is abnormal and reports it clearly to the people who decide.

Competency EZ01 describes the working conditions of that role: you work under supervision, together with other technicians and with the users of the network, and you are judged on professional ethics, on the management of priorities, on clear communication and on respect for the confidentiality of the data you collect. That last point matters: monitoring data shows what people do on their computers. It is confidential, it is used only for security, and it is never collected on a system you are not authorized to observe (Legal Notice, sections 7.2 and 9).

Real-life example 1 – The asset register of Grey Pine Bookkeeping

Slides 14 and 15 of the class presentation: Real-life example 1: the asset register; Rating scales: impact and exposure.

The task, and its relation with the lesson

Task: list what Grey Pine owns, estimate a simple risk score for each item, and order the items from the highest score to the lowest.

Relation with the lesson: the register applies the vocabulary just learned (asset, threat, vulnerability, risk) and answers the owner's question, why do we need cybersecurity, and where do we start?

Where you reuse it: the result is Deliverable 1. Lab 1 (Friday, September 25) starts from this register to map the network and to decide what must be monitored first.

The situation

The owner of Grey Pine reads in the news that one Canadian business in six was hit by a cyber security incident. She asks the technician who looks after the computers two questions: What do we have that needs protection? If we can watch only a few things closely, which ones come first? The technician works in four steps.

  1. List the assets. He walks through the office, asks each person which systems she uses, and reads the invoices of the online services. He finds eight assets and gives each one an identifier, from A1 to A8.
  2. Rate the impact from 1 to 5. The question is: what happens to the office if this asset is lost, disclosed or altered? He uses the scale of Table 3.
  3. Rate the exposure from 1 to 5. The question is: how easily can a threat reach this asset? Exposure stands for likelihood in this first estimate. He uses the scale of Table 3.
  4. Multiply and order. Score = impact × exposure. A score of 15 or more is in the high band, 8 to 14 in the medium band, 7 or less in the low band.

Two observations come out of the result. First, the file server has the highest impact but is only fourth, because it cannot be reached from the Internet; the e-mail accounts are more exposed and pass in front of it. Second, a score is a help for deciding, not a measurement. Two people can give different ratings. That is why the technician writes the reason for every rating; you will see these reasons as comments in the code below.

    The model
    Slide 21 of the class presentation: The three security properties.
    
      
    The definition of Lesson 1 listed six unwanted actions. They can be grouped under three properties that every piece of information, and every system, should keep. Together they are called the CIA (Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability) triad. The name has no relation with the intelligence agency of the United States; it is formed with the first letters of the three words.
    
      
    
        
    PropertyThe question it answersLosing it meansUnwanted actions of the definition
    
        
          
    ConfidentialityWho is able to read this?Unauthorized disclosureaccess, use, disclosure
    
          
    IntegrityIs this exact and complete, and was every change authorized?Unauthorized modification or destructionmodification, destruction
    
          
    AvailabilityCan authorized people use this when they need it?Disruption of access or of usedisruption, destruction
    
            
      
    
      
    The model is used by the standards you will meet throughout the program. The ISO (International Organization for Standardization)/IEC (International Electrotechnical Commission) 27000 family of standards defines information security as the preservation of these three properties. In the United States, the standard FIPS (Federal Information Processing Standards) 199 names them the three security objectives and uses them to classify every federal information system. The first performance criterion of our competency uses the same three words.
    
      
    The practical value of the model is that it gives you three questions to ask about any alert: was something read, was something changed, or was something made unreachable? The answer tells you whom to inform and how urgent the matter is.
    
    
    

    
    
      
    Confidentiality
    Slide 22 of the class presentation: Confidentiality.
    
      
    Confidentiality is the property that information is not made available or disclosed to persons, programs or organizations that are not authorized to have it. A loss of confidentiality is an unauthorized disclosure.
    
      
    Situations at Grey Pine that break confidentiality: a bookkeeper sends a tax return to the wrong client; a laptop whose disk is not encrypted is stolen from a car; a former employee still has a working account; a criminal obtains a password through a fraudulent e-mail and reads the mailbox for three weeks. Notice that in the last case nothing is changed and nothing stops working. A loss of confidentiality is often silent. Only monitoring reveals it.
    
      
    Controls that protect confidentiality
    
      
      
        
    • Authentication: the proof that a person or a program is who it claims to be. A password is one proof. MFA (multi-factor authentication) asks for two proofs of different kinds, for example a password and a code generated by a telephone.
      • 
        
    • Authorization and access control: the rules that state what each authenticated person may read or change. The principle of least privilege gives each person only the rights required for her work, and nothing more.
      • 
        
    • Encryption: the transformation of readable data into unreadable data with a mathematical algorithm and a key; only the holder of the right key can reverse it. Data is encrypted at rest (on a disk) and in transit (on a network).
      • 
        
    • Administrative controls: classification of information, training of the staff, a procedure that closes the accounts of departing employees on the same day.
      • 
      
    
      
    Privacy is a related but different idea. Privacy is the right of a person to control the information about herself; it is defined by laws such as Law 25. Confidentiality is a technical property that applies to any sensitive information, personal or not. Protecting confidentiality is one of the means of respecting privacy.
    
      
    What monitoring observes
    
      
    Failed and successful sign-ins, with the time and the origin; access to folders marked as sensitive; changes of permissions; new accounts, and above all new administrator accounts; unusually large transfers of data toward the outside.
    
    
    

    
    
      
    Integrity
    Slides 23 and 24 of the class presentation: Integrity; How a change is detected: the hash.
    
      
    Integrity is the property that information is exact and complete, and that information and systems are changed only by authorized persons, in authorized ways. A loss of integrity is an unauthorized modification or destruction. The definition used by the NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) places two more ideas under integrity: authenticity, the assurance that information really comes from the stated source, and non-repudiation, the impossibility for the author of an action to deny it later.
    
      
    Situations at Grey Pine that break integrity: an amount is altered in an invoice; a payee is added to the payroll file without approval; malicious software replaces a system file; an employee overwrites the current version of a spreadsheet with an old one by mistake; somebody erases lines of a log to hide an action. The fourth case shows that a loss of integrity does not require an attacker. The technician observes the change; the investigation establishes the cause.
    
      
    How a change is detected: the hash
    
      
    A hash function is a mathematical function that receives data of any size and computes a short value of fixed size, called the hash of the data. The function used most often today is SHA-256 (Secure Hash Algorithm, 256-bit); its result has 256 bits and is written as 64 hexadecimal characters. A hash function suitable for security has four properties:
    
      
      
        
    1. It is deterministic: the same input always gives the same hash.
      2. 
        
    2. Its output has a fixed length, whatever the size of the input: one line of text or a file of 4 gigabytes both give 64 characters.
      3. 
        
    3. A change of a single character of the input gives a completely different hash.
      4. 
        
    4. It works in one direction only: it is not feasible to rebuild the input from the hash, nor to construct two different inputs that have the same hash.
      5. 
      
    
      
    These properties give a simple method. When a file is in its approved state, compute its hash and record it. This record is a baseline: a reference of the normal state, kept to be compared with later observations. Later, compute the hash again. If the two values are equal, not a single byte has changed. If they differ, the content has changed.
    
      
    Be exact about what this proves. A different hash proves that the content changed after the baseline. It does not say who changed it, when, what was changed or whether the change was approved. Those answers come from the event logs and from the people concerned. A hash is also not encryption: it hides nothing, and the file remains readable. A tool that recomputes hashes on a schedule and raises an alert when one differs performs FIM (file integrity monitoring). You will configure this kind of check in Lab 2.
    
      
    Other controls that protect integrity
    
      
    Write permissions limited to the persons who need them; digital signatures, which combine a hash with a secret key to prove the author of a document; version history; a change management procedure, in which every modification of a system is requested, approved and recorded; and backups, which allow the return to a correct version.
    
    
    

    
    
      
    Availability
    Slides 25 and 26 of the class presentation: Availability; How availability is measured.
    
      
    Availability is the property that information and systems can be reached and used by authorized persons when they need them, within an acceptable time. A loss of availability is a disruption of access or of use.
    
      
    Situations at Grey Pine that break availability: ransomware encrypts the client files; the Internet link is saturated by a DDoS (distributed denial of service) attack, in which thousands of computers send requests at the same time; the disk of the file server is full and nobody can save; the power fails; an update is installed in the middle of the tax season and the accounting application no longer starts. Three of these five cases involve no attacker. Availability problems are sometimes security incidents and sometimes ordinary operational problems. Telling them apart is a skill of the monitoring technician, and it is the subject of sub-topic 1.5 on Saturday.
    
      
    How availability is measured
    
      
      
        
    • Uptime is the percentage of time during which a system answers normally. The percentages look close to one another, but the hours they leave are very different, as Table 6 shows.
      • 
        
    • The RTO (recovery time objective) is the longest time that the organization accepts to remain without a system after an interruption.
      • 
        
    • The RPO (recovery point objective) is the largest amount of recent work, expressed as a duration, that the organization accepts to lose. An RPO (recovery point objective) of 24 hours requires at least one backup per day.
      •

    Controls that protect availability
    
      
    Backups that are tested, following the 3-2-1 rule: three copies of the data, on two different kinds of storage, with one copy outside the office. A UPS (uninterruptible power supply), a battery unit that keeps the equipment running during a short power failure. Duplicate equipment for what cannot stop. Updates installed at a planned time and tested first. Enough disk, memory and network capacity for the busiest period. A written plan that says who does what when a system stops.
    
      
    What monitoring observes
    
      
    Whether each device answers; whether each service answers; the level of use of the processor, the memory, the disk and the network link; the result of every backup; the remaining validity of certificates and licences. Most of these signals announce a problem hours or days before it stops the work. This is preventative monitoring in its simplest form.

    The properties compete with one another
    
      
    A control that strengthens one property often weakens another. Every extra proof of identity protects confidentiality, and it slows down or sometimes blocks a legitimate user, which reduces availability. A password shared by the whole staff makes the application quickly available to everybody and destroys confidentiality and the ability to know who did what. There is no universal setting. Each organization decides, asset by asset, which property matters most.
    
      
    Rating the impact: low, moderate or high
    
      
    The standard FIPS (Federal Information Processing Standards) 199 proposes a method that small organizations can borrow. For each asset, ask three separate questions, what is the effect on the organization if confidentiality is lost? if integrity is lost? if availability is lost?, and answer each with one of three levels.
    
      
      
        
    • Low: the effect is limited. The work continues with a minor loss.
      • 
        
    • Moderate: the effect is serious. An important activity is degraded for a significant time, or the financial loss is significant.
      • 
        
    • High: the effect is severe or catastrophic. The organization cannot carry out its main activity, or persons suffer serious harm.
      • 
      
    
      
    The overall level of the asset is the highest of its three levels. This rule is called the high-water mark. An asset rated high for confidentiality, moderate for integrity and low for availability is a high-impact asset, because one serious weakness is enough to cause serious harm.     Real-life example 2 – One payroll file, three checks
    Slide 30 of the class presentation: Real-life example 2: one payroll file, three checks.
    
      
    
        The task, and its relation with the lesson
        
    Task: protect one sensitive file of Grey Pine, the payroll export of September, by checking its three properties: who can read it, whether it changed since its approval, and whether it can be reached.
    
        
    Relation with the lesson: each check is one property of the triad turned into a concrete, measurable test. The example also shows the method of the baseline and its limits.
    
        
    Where you reuse it: the ratings of Table 10 and the baseline record of Table 11 form Deliverable 2. Lab 1 uses the ratings to choose what to monitor first. Lab 2 turns the baseline into an alert (the hash of a protected file changed). Lab 3 gives you an anomaly of this kind to diagnose and to report.
    
      
    
      
    
        The situation
        
    On September 15 the owner of Grey Pine approves the payroll export, the file payroll_2026-09.csv, stored on the file server. It lists three employees and their net pay. The technician does two things the same day. He sets the permissions so that only the owner and the payroll group can open the file, and he records the hash of the approved file: this is the baseline.
    
        
    On September 17, during his routine check, he runs the same three tests again. Two of them fail. The permissions now allow every user of the server to read the file, and the hash is different: the file has a fourth row, a payee that nobody recognizes, with an amount of 9,999 dollars.
    
        
    What does he do? He does not delete the row, and he does not restore the permissions on his own. He records the evidence (the two hashes, the two permission values, the time of the check), he informs the owner immediately, and he keeps the file as it is for the analysis. Correcting silently would destroy the evidence and hide a possible fraud. The Code of Ethics of this course asks the same thing: report findings accurately and completely, and never conceal an incident (Legal Notice, section 9).
    
      
    
    
    

    
    
      
    Code example 2 – The three checks in Bash
    Slide 31 of the class presentation: Code example 2: three checks in Bash.
    
      
    
        What this program does, and its relation with the lesson
        
    Task of the program: it creates the fictional payroll file in a working folder, sets its permissions, records the baseline hash, and runs three checks: confidentiality (can other users read the file?), integrity (does the hash equal the baseline?) and availability (is the file readable, and is the disk less than 90 percent full?). It then simulates the unauthorized change and runs the three checks a second time.
    
        
    Relation with the lesson: one function, check_cia, contains one test per property. While it runs, the triangle of the visualization shows which property holds and which one is lost, with the evidence beside it.

    77    echo "A  FAIL  file unreadable or disk almost full - disk ${used}% used"
    78  # End of the availability test.
    79  fi

      Why a separate lab
      Slide 36 of the class presentation: Why a separate lab.
      
      
      A monitoring tool reads logs, captures network traffic and uses processor time, memory and disk space. There are three reasons to practise with such tools in a separate environment and never on a network that people use for their work.
      
      
        
        
      1. A legal reason. Observing a system or capturing traffic is lawful only on systems that you own, or that the owner has authorized you, in writing, to work on. On a shared network, a capture records the communications of other people. The Legal Notice of this course (sections 6.1 and 7.2) explains the rule and the offences of the Criminal Code that apply.
        2. 
        
      2. A technical reason. An exercise can fill a disk, stop a service or make a system unusable. In a lab, the damaged machine is erased and rebuilt in a few minutes, and nobody loses any work.
        3. 
        
      3. A teaching reason. When every student has the same machines with the same names and addresses, results can be compared, and the instructor can find an error quickly.
        4.

An Authorized Lab Environment is an isolated environment that (a) you own or are expressly permitted to use for the exercise, (b) is disconnected from, or securely separated from, any production, institutional, corporate, public or third-party network, and (c) contains no real personal data and no system that belongs to a person who has not consented in writing.       How virtualization works
      Slides 37 to 39 of the class presentation: How virtualization works; Virtual disk, installation image, snapshot, clone; What your computer needs.
      
      
      Virtualization is a technique in which software divides the resources of one physical computer, namely the processor, the memory, the disk and the network connection, among several isolated environments. Each environment behaves as an independent computer.
      
      
        
        
      • A virtual machine is one of these environments. It has a virtual processor, virtual memory, a virtual disk and a virtual network card, and it runs its own operating system.
        • 
        
      • A hypervisor is the software that creates the virtual machines, runs them and shares the physical resources among them. A type 1 hypervisor is installed directly on the hardware, without any other operating system; data centres and cloud providers use this type. A type 2 hypervisor is installed as an ordinary application on Windows, macOS or Linux. VirtualBox and VMware Workstation Pro are type 2 hypervisors.
        • 
        
      • The host is the physical computer with its operating system. A guest is an operating system that runs inside a virtual machine.
        • 
        
      • A virtual disk is a large file on the host. For the guest, that file is a complete hard disk. A virtual machine is therefore easy to copy, to move and to delete.
        • 
        
      • An installation image is a file, usually with the extension .iso, that contains an exact copy of an installation disc. You attach it to the virtual machine to install the guest. Download it only from the official site of the publisher, then compute its SHA-256 (Secure Hash Algorithm, 256-bit) hash and compare it with the value that the publisher displays. This is the integrity check of Lesson 2, applied to your own downloads.
        • 
        
      • A snapshot is a recorded state of a virtual machine at a given moment. You can return to that state later. Take a snapshot before every exercise. A snapshot is not a backup: it is stored on the same disk as the virtual machine, so a failure of that disk destroys both.
        • 
        
      • A clone is an independent copy of a virtual machine, with its own name. A common method is to install one clean Linux machine and to clone it once for each role of the lab.
        • 
      
      
      
      Two facts about the host
      
      
      The processor must allow virtualization. Modern processors contain a feature that lets the hypervisor run the instructions of the guest directly on the processor. Intel names it VT-x (Virtualization Technology for x86 processors); AMD (Advanced Micro Devices) names it AMD-V (AMD Virtualization). The feature is switched on or off in the firmware settings of the computer, the UEFI (Unified Extensible Firmware Interface) settings, which many people still call by the older name BIOS (Basic Input/Output System). When the feature is off, a 64-bit virtual machine does not start. On Windows, the Task Manager shows the state: tab Performance, processor section, line Virtualization. Microsoft explains how to switch it on in Enable virtualization on Windows.
      
      
      Resources are shared, not created. The memory that you give to a virtual machine is taken from the host for as long as the machine runs. Three virtual machines with 4, 2 and 4 gigabytes use 10 gigabytes; the host needs the rest. Table 12 gives the requirements of this course.

It only reads information. It changes nothing on the computer and sends nothing anywhere.

By default Windows refuses to run script files; the option -ExecutionPolicy Bypass lifts that refusal for this single run and does not change the setting of the computer.

      22# Define a function that adds one line to the list; it receives the name of the check, the measured value, the requirement and a true/false verdict.
      23function Add-Check {
      24    # Declare the four inputs of the function and their types, so a wrong kind of value is refused at once.
      25    param([string]$Check, [string]$Measured, [string]$Required, [bool]$Ok)
      26    # Translate the true/false verdict into the word PASS or FAIL, which is easier to read in a report.
      27    $status = if ($Ok) { 'PASS' } else { 'FAIL' }
      28    # Build one result object with four named properties and append it to the list that belongs to the whole script.
      29    $script:results += [PSCustomObject]@{ Check = $Check; Measured = $Measured; Required = $Required; Status = $status }
      30# End of the function definition.
      31}

      34$computer = Get-CimInstance -ClassName Win32_ComputerSystem
      35

      37$ramGB = [math]::Round($computer.TotalPhysicalMemory / 1GB)
      38

      40Add-Check -Check 'Installed memory' -Measured "$ramGB GB" -Required "$minRamGB GB or more" -Ok ($ramGB -ge $minRamGB)

      43$processor = Get-CimInstance -ClassName Win32_Processor | Select-Object -First 1

        Key points of today
        Slide 50 of the class presentation: Key points of today.
        
      
          
        
        1. Cybersecurity protects computers, networks, programs and digital information against six unauthorized actions: access, use, disclosure, disruption, modification and destruction.
          2. 
        
        2. A risk combines the likelihood that a threat uses a vulnerability with the impact if it happens. No organization removes every risk; the highest risks are treated first.
          3. 
        
        3. An event is observed, an alert is produced by a rule, and an incident is established by analysis. Preventative monitoring detects and reports problems before they cause damage.
          4. 
        
        4. Organizations of every size need cybersecurity: in 2023, one Canadian business in six was impacted, recovery spending doubled, and monitoring was the security activity reported most often.
          5. 
        
        5. Every alert leads to three questions: was something read, was something changed, was something made unreachable? These are confidentiality, integrity and availability.
          6. 
        
        6. A hash compared with a baseline proves that the content changed. It does not say who changed it, when or why. The technician reports the evidence and does not repair in silence.
          7. 
        
        7. Each asset is rated low, moderate or high for each property, and the highest of the three levels is its overall level.
          8. 
        
        8. Lab work runs in virtual machines on a host-only network, with a snapshot before each exercise and with fictional data only. The bridged mode and captures on shared networks are excluded.

        9. The program uses four environments: VirtualBox, VMware Workstation Pro, the AWS (Amazon Web Services) Academy Learner Lab and, later, the CyberQuébec range.
