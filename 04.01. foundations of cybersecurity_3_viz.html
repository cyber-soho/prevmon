1.5.1 Five words that must mean the same thing for everyone

Slides 7–8

Monitoring produces disagreements when two people use the same word for two different things. Before any classification method can work, the vocabulary has to be fixed. These five definitions are used for the rest of the course, in the labs, and in the examinations.

Event
Any observable occurrence in a system or on a network: a user signing in, a file being written, a service restarting, a packet crossing an interface. An event is neutral. It is not good or bad by itself.
Alert
A notification produced by a monitoring tool when an event, or a measurement, matches a rule or crosses a threshold that a human being configured in advance. An alert is a claim made by a tool, not a fact.
Incident
An event, or a group of connected events, that harms or threatens to harm the confidentiality, integrity or availability of information or of a service, or that breaks a policy. An incident is a conclusion reached by a person after looking at evidence.
Baseline (reference database of normal activity)
A written and measured description of how a system or a network behaves when nothing is wrong, recorded over a period long enough to include the normal cycles of the organization: working hours, evenings, week-ends, month-end. Without a baseline, the words "high", "slow" and "unusual" have no meaning.
False positive and false negative
A false positive is an alert raised when nothing abnormal happened. A false negative is the absence of an alert when something abnormal did happen. Both are failures of the monitoring configuration, and both are reduced by a good baseline.

Order of the three words in practice: a tool observes an event, a rule turns some events into an alert, and a human being decides whether the alert describes an incident. The work you are learning in this course lives almost entirely in the third step.

1.5.2 What a performance issue is

Slides 9–10

A performance issue is a condition in which a system or a service still behaves exactly as it was designed to behave, but delivers its function too slowly, with less capacity than required, or with interruptions, because of demand, resource limits, faults, or configuration. No unauthorized person is involved.

The usual causes are ordinary and well known:

  • Demand higher than the capacity that was planned: more users, larger files, a new report that runs at nine o'clock.
  • Exhaustion of a resource: CPU (Central Processing Unit) time, RAM (Random Access Memory), disk space, disk input and output, or network bandwidth.
  • A fault in hardware: a failing disk, a defective cable, a network interface negotiating at the wrong speed.
  • A change made by the organization itself: an update, a new backup schedule, a modified configuration, a new application.
  • A design limit: a single link, a single server, a service that cannot use more than one processor core.

The evidence of a performance issue is quantitative and repeatable. Values rise and fall with the load. The problem follows a schedule, or it follows the number of active users, or it started at the same moment as a documented change.

1.5.3 What a security issue is

Slides 11–13

A security issue is a condition caused by, or indicating, an action that was not authorized, an attempt at such an action, or a weakness that makes such an action possible. It affects at least one of the three properties you studied in subject 1.2: confidentiality, integrity, availability.

The distinguishing element is not severity and not slowness. It is the presence of an actor: a person, a script, or a program acting outside the authorization given to it. That actor leaves traces that a machine fault never leaves:

  • Authentication records that do not match any legitimate working pattern: repeated failures, then one success, at an hour when the office is empty.
  • New accounts, new group memberships, new permissions, new services, new scheduled tasks that no change record explains.
  • Files created, renamed or encrypted in volume by a single account or a single process.
  • Connections to destinations that appear nowhere in the baseline, especially outbound connections carrying volume.
  • Records that are missing: cleared logs, gaps in a log file, a monitoring agent that stopped reporting on its own.
  • Software that was installed without an installation record, or a file whose cryptographic hash no longer matches the reference value.

The point that decides most cases

A performance issue is explained by demand, faults and change. A security issue is explained by an actor. When you cannot explain a symptom by demand, by a fault or by a documented change, and when traces of an actor are present, the classification is security — even when the service is still working perfectly.

1.5.4 Why the two look alike on the first screen

Slides 14–15

Users report symptoms, not causes. The report you receive is almost always one of four sentences: it is slow, it stopped, it is full, or it is behaving strangely. Each of those sentences has a routine explanation and a hostile explanation, and the first screen of a monitoring tool rarely separates them.

Same symptom, two possible origins, and the evidence that decides between them
Reported symptomRoutine explanation (performance)Hostile explanation (security)Evidence that decides
The shared application is slow every morning All staff sign in within ten minutes; a report runs at the same hour Automated password guessing against the sign-in page, or a DDoS (Distributed Denial of Service) attack Number of requests per source address; ratio of failed to successful sign-ins; whether the sources appear in the baseline
The file server disk is filling quickly A backup job keeps more copies than expected; logging was set to a higher level of detail Ransomware writing encrypted copies, or data being gathered in one folder before being sent out Which process writes; which account owns the new files; the file extensions; the write rate compared with the baseline
The internet link is saturated in the evening Operating system updates, video conferencing, large legitimate transfers Exfiltration of data, or a compromised host distributing traffic Direction of the traffic, destination addresses, protocol, session duration, volume per host
One workstation runs at full processor load A runaway application, or an antivirus full scan Cryptocurrency mining software installed without permission Name and path of the process, whether it is digitally signed, persistence entries, outbound destinations
A service restarts repeatedly A defective driver, unstable power, a memory leak An actor crashing the service deliberately, or an unauthorized configuration change Who or what issued the restart; correlation with sign-in records; configuration file modification times

Two further facts complete the picture, and both are examinable.

  • A security incident can produce a performance symptom. Encryption of a file share, a DoS (Denial of Service) attack and mining software all consume resources. The slowness is real; it is simply not the cause.
  • A security incident can produce no performance symptom at all. Copying a client folder to a personal cloud account, creating a hidden administrator account, or reading a database that the actor has no right to read costs the network nothing measurable. This is why monitoring cannot be limited to performance counters.

1.5.5 The four-question triage

Slides 16–22

Triage means sorting reports and alerts in order to decide which ones require action and in what order. The four questions below are asked in the same sequence every time. Each one is answered with data you can point at, not with an opinion.

The four questions, the data that answers each one, and what the answer tells you
#QuestionWhere the answer comes fromWhat it establishes
1What exactly is affected, and since when? The report itself, the asset inventory, the monitoring history, the times recorded in the logs Scope and start time. One host or all hosts, one service or the whole link, a single moment or a daily pattern.
2Does demand or a change explain it? Number of active users and sessions, scheduled jobs, backup windows, update history, the change record Whether the measurement follows normal work. If it rises and falls with the load, or begins with a documented change, the explanation is ordinary.
3Is there evidence of an actor? Authentication logs, account and group changes, new services and scheduled tasks, process lists, outbound connections, file creation patterns, integrity checks Whether something acted outside its authorization. This is the question that decides most cases.
4What does the baseline say? The reference database of normal activity for this asset, at this hour, on this day of the week Whether the value is outside the normal range. Without this answer, questions 2 and 3 are guesses.

The decision rule

  1. Traces of an actor are present, and no authorization explains them → Security Treat as a security incident until the evidence disproves it.
  2. No trace of an actor, and demand, a fault or a documented change explains the values, and the values stay inside the baseline for this time of week → Performance
  3. Traces of an actor are present and a resource is exhausted → Both Handle the security path first; the resource problem is a consequence.
  4. The evidence does not answer question 3 → Undetermined Preserve the data, raise the priority, escalate. Never close a case as "just slow" because no one found the cause.

The reason the rule is asymmetric is the cost of the two possible mistakes. Calling a security incident a performance problem leaves an actor inside the network with time to continue. Calling a performance problem a security incident costs an escalation and some wasted hours. The second mistake is corrected in a morning; the first one is corrected by a lawyer.

1.5.6 What each verdict obliges you to do

Slides 23–24

The classification is not a label for a report. It selects a procedure, an audience, and a set of legal duties.

Verdict, immediate action, and the people who must be informed
VerdictImmediate actionWho is informed, and what is recorded
Performance Measure, identify the constrained resource, plan a correction, schedule it as a change The service owner and the operations team. Recorded as a service request with the measurements attached.
Security Preserve the evidence before changing anything: export the relevant logs, note exact times, do not reboot, do not delete, do not "clean" the machine. Then follow the organization's incident procedure. The person named in the incident procedure. In Québec, where personal information is involved, an enterprise must record every confidentiality incident in a register and, where the incident presents a risk of serious injury, notify the CAI (Commission d'accès à l'information du Québec) and the persons concerned.
Both Run the security procedure first, and keep the performance measurements as evidence of impact Both audiences, with one single timeline of facts so that the two teams do not contradict each other.
Undetermined Preserve the data, widen the collection, set a temporary alert, and set a review time Your supervisor, with a written statement of what is missing and what you propose to collect next.

Two obligations from the Cyber.SoHo rules apply to everything you do in this course, and they are not optional: capture data only on systems you own or that the laboratory guide designates, and treat every capture, log file and baseline as personal information about the people who used those systems. Keep the minimum, store it in the laboratory, delete it when the exercise is marked. The full rule is section 7.2 of the Cyber.SoHo Legal Notice, Terms of Use and Acceptable Use Policy.

1.5.7 Five mistakes beginners make, and the correction

Slides 25–27
MistakeWhy it fails, and what to do instead
Deciding from the severity of the symptomSeverity measures impact, not origin. A completely stopped service is often a failed disk; a service that runs perfectly is often the one being read by an intruder. Decide from question 3.
Rebooting or reimaging firstA restart destroys memory contents, running process lists and temporary files. If the verdict turns out to be security, the evidence is gone and the timeline cannot be rebuilt. Export first, act second.
Comparing against a feeling instead of a baseline"That looks high" is not a measurement. Record the baseline first, in subject 3.4 and in Lab 2, then compare against it.
Treating one alert as one incidentA single alert is one tool's opinion. Incidents are built from several sources that agree: a sign-in record, a process, a network connection, a file change.
Closing an unexplained case"It went away" is not a diagnosis. Unexplained cases become Undetermined with a review date, not closed tickets.

Two reports on the same week-end at Atelier Nord-Ouest

Slides 29–35

Task performed by this example: take two ordinary user reports from a small business network, apply the four-question triage to each one, reach a classification supported by named evidence, and write the result on a triage sheet.

Relation to this lecture: it is subject 1.5 executed end to end. Report A has an ordinary explanation, report B has a hostile one, and both begin with the same sentence from the user: "it is slow". The example also exercises criterion 1.4 of competency EZ01, the clear distinction between performance and security issues.

Deliverable and where it is used again: the completed triage sheet becomes Appendix A of the monitoring plan you submit for Lab 1 on 25 September, because it justifies which assets you rank first. The threshold you propose in the last column becomes an alert value you configure in Lab 2 on 3 October. The same sheet is the reporting form you fill in for the anomaly of Lab 3 on 16 October.

Atelier Nord-Ouest is a fictional design and print studio in Montréal with fourteen employees. It is the case used for the whole integrative project. Its network: one router with a firewall (RTR01) on a 300/100 Mbps (Megabits per second) fibre connection, one unmanaged switch (SW01), one wireless access point (AP01), one Windows file server (FS01) holding client projects, one Linux backup server (BK01), twelve workstations, one large-format plotter (PLT01) and one office printer (PRN01). Business hours are Monday to Saturday, 8 a.m. to 6 p.m. The backup runs every night at 01:00. No staff work on Sunday.

Classification. Report A is a Performance issue: demand and a documented change explain it, the values sit inside the Saturday baseline, and there is no actor. The correction is a scheduling decision, taken with the service owner. Report B is a Security incident that also produces a performance symptom, which makes it a Both case in the reporting: the security procedure runs first, and the processor and disk measurements are kept as evidence of impact. Because client files are involved, the personal information rules apply, and the incident is recorded in the register of confidentiality incidents.

What you must not do with report B. Do not restart FS01, do not delete the new files, and do not run a cleaning tool. Export the security log, the file listing with times, and the firewall connection record first. The cleared log at 02:31 is itself evidence: it is an action, and actions have authors.

    1.1 What cybersecurity is, and why an organization needs it
    Slide 39
    

    Cybersecurity is the set of practices, technical measures and organizational rules that
    protect information, the systems that process it, and the services built on them, against unauthorized access,
    unauthorized modification, disruption and destruction. It is not a product and it is not a department. It is a
    property of how an organization works.
    

    Organizations need it for four reasons that come up again in every later topic:
    

      

    • Operational. Work stops when systems stop. A studio that cannot open its project files cannot deliver.
      • 

    • Financial. Interruption, recovery, replacement and lost contracts cost more than the measures that prevent them.
      • 

    • Legal. In Québec an enterprise that holds personal information has obligations under the
      Act respecting the protection of personal information in the private sector, as amended by Law 25: protect the
      information, keep a register of confidentiality incidents, and notify the
      CAI (Commission d'accès à l'information du Québec) and the persons concerned when an incident presents
      a risk of serious injury.
      • 

    • Reputational. Clients hand over their drawings, their records and their money on the assumption that both are safe.
      • 

    

    Monitoring is the part of cybersecurity that makes the other parts visible. Without observation, an organization
    does not know whether its measures work, and it learns about incidents from the outside, usually late.

    1.4 Cyber defence concepts: prevention, detection, response
    Slide 43
    

    Defence is organized in three moments, and every control belongs to one of them.
    

    

    Prevention
    Measures that stop an unwanted action from succeeding: authentication, including
      MFA (Multi-Factor Authentication), permissions, firewall rules, network segmentation, updates,
      hardening, and training.
    

    Detection
    Measures that make an action visible while or shortly after it happens: logging, monitoring tools,
      IDS (Intrusion Detection System), SIEM (Security Information and Event Management),
      file integrity checking, alerting on a baseline deviation. This is where this course lives.
    

    Response
    Measures that limit the damage and restore service: isolating a host, disabling an account,
      blocking an address, restoring from backup, and the incident procedure that says who decides and who is told.
    

    

    Controls are also grouped by nature: technical (a firewall rule), administrative or managerial
    (a policy, an access review), operational (a procedure carried out by people, such as log review), and
    physical (a lock on the server cabinet). Defence in depth is the practice of placing
    several independent controls on the same path, so that one failure does not become an incident.
    

    

    The sentence to remember for the examination: detection exists because prevention fails. No set of
      preventive measures is complete, which is why an organization that monitors nothing is an organization that will
      learn about its incidents from a client, a bank, or a journalist.
    

    

    

    

    1.5 Security issues versus performance issues, in one paragraph
    Slide 44
    

    A performance issue is explained by demand, a fault or a documented change; a security issue is explained by an
    actor. The four questions establish scope and time, then demand and change, then actor evidence, then distance from the
    baseline. The verdict is one of four — performance, security, both, undetermined — and it selects the procedure, the
    audience, and what must be preserved. When the evidence is silent on question 3, the verdict is undetermined and the
    case stays open.

    Scoring rules. Impact is what the business loses if the asset fails or is misused: 3 if work stops or
      client information is exposed, 2 if work is slowed, 1 if the effect is minor. Exposure is how easily something
      can reach the asset: 3 if it is reachable from the internet or from an untrusted network, 2 if every internal user
      reaches it daily, 1 if access is restricted. Score is impact multiplied by exposure. Tier 1 is a score of 6 or
      more, Tier 2 is 3 to 5, Tier 3 is 2 or less. In the inventory file the two columns are written as the words High, Medium and Low, because that is easier for a business owner to fill in; the script converts them to 3, 2 and 1. The rule is written down so that the ranking can be defended in front of
      the owner of the business, which is exactly what criterion 2.3 of the competency will ask of you in Topic 2.

      What the script does, step by step
      

        

      1. It reads the inventory file. Import-Csv turns every line of the file into an object whose properties carry the
        names written in the first line of the file: Asset, Role, Property, LikelyAttack, Impact, Exposure.
        2. 

      2. It builds two small translation tables so that the words High, Medium and Low become the numbers 3, 2 and 1.
        Words are easier for people to fill in; numbers are needed for the calculation.
        3. 

      3. It creates an empty list that will hold the scored assets.
        4. 

      4. It goes through the assets one by one. For each one it reads the two words, converts them to numbers, and
        multiplies them to obtain the score.
        5. 

      5. It converts the score into a tier with a simple rule: 6 or more is Tier 1, 3 to 5 is Tier 2, anything else is
        Tier 3. The rule is written in the script so that the ranking can be explained and repeated.
        6. 

      6. It adds the asset, with its score and its tier, to the list, and prints one line so that you can follow the
        calculation while it runs.
        7. 

      7. It sorts the finished list from the highest score to the lowest, so that the first line of the output is the
        asset to instrument first.
        8. 

      8. It writes the sorted list to a new file with Export-Csv, and prints a summary showing how many assets fall in
        each tier. That file is the attachment for Lab 1.
