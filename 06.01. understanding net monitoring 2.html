Slides 8–31Competency EZ01, element 2, performance criterion 2.1: thorough examination of the organizational and operational constraints of the network

2.3.1 What a constraint is, and why it comes before any tool Slides 9–10

In Class 5 you produced a map of the network. A map tells you what exists. It does not tell you what you are allowed to do with it, what the equipment is able to do, or how much the organization can afford. Those limits are the subject of this lesson.

ConstraintA constraint is a condition, imposed by the organization or by the way its network operates, that limits what monitoring can be done, how it can be done, when, where, by whom, and at what cost. A constraint is a fact, not an opinion: it can be traced to a document, a contract, a law, a measurement or a decision of the owner.

Two families of constraints exist, and the competency asks you to examine both:

  • An organizational constraint comes from the organization as a business or institution: its money, its people, its rules, its contracts, the laws that apply to it, and its schedule.
  • An operational constraint comes from the technical way the network runs: the capacity of its links and disks, the capabilities of its equipment, the systems that cannot be stopped or modified, and the way traffic is segmented or encrypted.

A constraint is different from a requirement. A requirement states what monitoring must achieve (for example, “detect a failed backup within 12 hours”); requirements are the subject of Lesson 2.4. A constraint states what monitoring must respect while achieving it. A monitoring plan that ignores its constraints fails in one of three ways: it is refused by the owner, it breaks the network it was supposed to protect, or it breaks the law.

Hard constraint and soft constraintA hard constraint cannot be changed by the monitoring project itself: a law, a signed contract, a safety rule on a machine. A soft constraint could be removed later with money, time or a decision: an unmanaged switch that could be replaced, a budget that could be increased next year. Both are recorded; only the soft ones become recommendations in Lesson 6.5 (Recommending tools and improvements).

2.3.3 Operational constraints Slides 14–17

Operational constraints are found by looking at the equipment, reading its specifications and measuring. Most of them have a number attached, and the number is what goes in the register.

CategoryWhat limits monitoringExample at Val-des-Pins (fictional)
Link capacity (bandwidth)Monitoring traffic (log forwarding, polling, mirrored traffic) shares the links with production traffic. A mirrored copy of a busy link can exceed what the capture port can receive.Internet link 100 Mbit/s; office and workshop connected by one 100 Mbit/s cable; the plan sets a ceiling of 5 Mbit/s for monitoring traffic.
Storage capacityEvery log entry and every captured packet occupies disk space for the whole retention period. Storage needed = bytes per event × events per second × 86,400 seconds per day × retention days.SRV-FILE-01 is 85 % full and cannot receive logs; NAS-01 has 1.2 TB (terabytes) free. 25 firewall events per second of 300 bytes each, kept 90 days, need about 58 GB (gigabytes).
Processing capacity of hostsA monitoring agent (a program installed on a computer to collect its logs or metrics) uses CPU (Central Processing Unit) time and RAM (Random Access Memory) on that computer.The office workstations can take a light agent; the file server is old and must not lose more than 5 % of its processor capacity.
Availability requirementsSome systems cannot be stopped, rebooted or modified except during a maintenance window agreed with the owner.CNC-PC-01 cannot be touched while a job runs (up to 6 hours); the file server can be restarted only after 17:00 with notice.
Legacy and unsupported systemsOld operating systems and embedded devices (printers, machine controllers, cameras) often accept no agent, produce no usable log, and support only old protocols.CNC-PC-01 runs Windows 7, which its publisher no longer supports; PRN-01 produces no log at all.
Features of the network equipmentPort mirroring (SPAN (Switched Port Analyzer), which copies the traffic of one port to another for capture), SNMP (Simple Network Management Protocol, used to read counters from equipment), and log export (syslog) exist only on equipment that supports them.SW-01 supports SPAN and SNMP; SW-02 is unmanaged and supports none of them; FW-01 exports its log by syslog.
Segmentation and filteringFirewalls and VLANs (Virtual Local Area Networks) separate the network into zones; monitoring traffic must be allowed through them (for example syslog on UDP (User Datagram Protocol) port 514, SNMP on UDP ports 161 and 162). Remote sites and users reach the network over a WAN (Wide Area Network) link or a VPN.The workshop and the office are separate networks joined through FW-01; the sales laptop is reachable only when its VPN is up.
Encryption and cloud servicesTraffic protected by TLS (Transport Layer Security) shows who talks to whom, when and how much, but not the content. For a cloud service, only what the provider exposes (audit logs, status pages) can be monitored.E-mail and accounting are cloud services: the plan relies on the provider's sign-in audit log, not on packet content.
Time synchronizationEvents from different devices can be compared only if their clocks agree. NTP (Network Time Protocol) keeps clocks synchronized.The firewall and the file server differ by 4 minutes; NTP must be enabled on every device before logs are correlated (Lab 2).
Physical and environmentalWhere the collector sits, its power, its cooling and who can physically reach it.The only rack is in the office storage room; the workshop is dusty and has no protected outlet.
Security issue or performance issue? Lesson 1.5 taught you to tell them apart. Operational constraints are where the two meet: a monitoring setup that ignores link and disk capacity will itself create the performance problem it was meant to detect. Lesson 6.2 (Reducing the impact of monitoring on network performance) returns to this point.

2.3.4 Why constraints matter for monitoring Slide 18

  1. Monitoring consumes resources. Collecting, transporting and storing data costs bandwidth, disk, processor time and money. The operational constraints tell you how much you can spend of each.
  2. Monitoring collects data about people. A log of connections is a record of what employees and visitors did. The legal constraints tell you what you may collect, whom you must inform, and how long you may keep it.
  3. Monitoring changes systems. Installing an agent, enabling log export or configuring a mirror port is a change to a production system. The change-management constraint tells you when and how it may be done, and how to undo it.
  4. Monitoring without authorization is an offence, whatever the intention. Section 6.1 of the Legal Notice states the rule that governs everything else: you may apply these techniques only to systems you own or are authorized in writing to work on. The authorization constraint is therefore the first line of every register.
For the labs of this course. Your Authorized Lab Environment is the only network you monitor. You never enable promiscuous or monitor mode on a network you share with other people, you never install a collector on a device that belongs to someone else, and you never capture traffic on the college's or your home's Wi-Fi “to have real data”. Annex E of the Notice names this exact mistake as the most likely way to get this course wrong.

2.3.5 Examining the constraints and recording them: the constraint register Slides 19–20

The performance criterion says “thorough examination”. Thorough means you used every source available, not that you guessed carefully. Four sources exist:

  • Documentation: inventory, topology diagram, contracts, policies, provider terms (Lesson 2.2 taught you to read them).
  • Interviews: the owner (money, tolerance for disruption, authorization), the users (schedule, peak periods), the provider's technician (hours, skills), the vendor (what the contract forbids).
  • Observation: the model and features of each device, the free disk space, the link speeds, the presence of NTP.
  • Measurement: how many events per second a device produces, how much traffic crosses a link at the busiest hour. This is the beginning of the baseline you will build in Topic 3.

Everything you find goes into one table, the constraint register. Its columns are fixed so that every student's register can be compared with every other's:

ColumnWhat goes in it

Task

Deliverable and future labs

The situation. On Monday morning the technician sits with the owner for one hour. She confirms the budget (CAD 1,500), the provider's hours (6 per month) and the rule about guests. The technician then walks through the building: the NAS has 1.2 TB free, the workshop switch is a small unmanaged model, the CNC computer shows the vendor's sticker “do not install software”, and the firewall's menu has a syslog export option. Back at the desk, the technician lists what could be done:

  1. A0 — set up one small used computer as the collector (CAD 350, 1 hour per month to maintain);
  2. A1 — collect the Windows event logs of the file server and the six workstations (about 8 events per second, 900 bytes each);
  3. A2 — export the firewall connection log to the collector (about 25 events per second, 300 bytes each);
  4. A3 — availability checks every minute on the servers, CNC-PC-01, the printer and the Internet link;
  5. A4 — SNMP polling of SW-01 and FW-01 every 60 seconds;
  6. A5 — full packet capture of the Internet link, all day (a dedicated capture computer, CAD 900, and about 30 Mbit/s of data on average);
  7. A6 — a monitoring agent on CNC-PC-01;
  8. A7 — capture the guest Wi-Fi traffic.

The technician then applies the register: the budget (C-01), the hours minus a reserve of 1.5 hours for reacting to alerts (C-02), the free space on the NAS for a 90-day retention (C-07), the 5 Mbit/s ceiling (C-08), the no-software rule on CNC-PC-01 (C-04 and C-09) and the guest-network rule (C-05). Two activities get a lighter version: A5 becomes A5b, flow records (connection summaries that the firewall already produces) instead of every packet; A6 becomes A6b, availability checks plus the firewall log instead of an agent. A7 has no acceptable version and is refused. The program below performs exactly these checks.

2.4.1 What a monitoring need is Slides 33–34


Lesson 2.3 told us what we must respect. This lesson tells us what we must watch. The competency calls it a surveillance need; in this course we say monitoring need, the same thing.


Monitoring needA monitoring need is a precise statement of one thing that must be observed on one asset, for one security or operational reason, together with the sign that would reveal it, the place where that sign is recorded, and the time within which it must be noticed. A need is written before any tool is chosen; the tool comes afterwards, in Topic 3, to satisfy the need.


Five terms make a need precise. Learn them; the register, the quiz and the labs use them constantly.


    
  
  • Event: something that happens on a system and can be recorded: a logon, a connection, a file copied, a job that finishes, a device that stops answering.
    • 
  
  • Observable (also called an indicator): the recordable sign that shows the event happened, written with a number or a pattern so that two people would read it the same way: “10 or more failed logons from one source within 5 minutes”, not “too many failed logons”. When the event is an intrusion, the observable is an IoC (Indicator of Compromise).
    • 
  
  • Data source: where the observable is recorded: the Windows event log, the firewall connection log, an availability check, the antivirus console, the provider's sign-in audit log, the DHCP (Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol) lease log of the address server, SNMP counters, the nightly report of the NAS. Topic 4 studies how to read each of them.
    • 
  
  • Detection time objective: the longest acceptable delay between the event and the moment someone knows. The industry measures the delay actually achieved as the MTTD (Mean Time To Detect).
    • 
  
  • Criticality: how much the asset needs the requirement the event would harm, rated 1 (low) to 3 (high) for each of C (Confidentiality), I (Integrity) and A (Availability), the CIA (Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability) triad of Topic 1.
    • 



Need, alert, tool: three different things. The need is the requirement (“detect a failed backup within 12 hours”). The alert is the message a tool produces when the observable crosses its threshold (Lesson 3.5). The tool is the program that reads the data source and produces the alert (Lesson 3.2). Writing “install an antivirus” as a need is a common beginner's mistake: it names a tool and says nothing about what must be detected.







2.4.2 Where monitoring needs come from Slides 35–36


Needs are not invented at a desk. They come from six places, and a thorough identification visits all six.


    
  
  1. The security requirements of each asset. Topic 1 taught you to rate the confidentiality, integrity and availability that each asset needs. An asset rated 3 for confidentiality (the file server with the employee files) needs to be watched for anything that would disclose its data; an asset rated 3 for availability (the CNC computer during a job) needs to be watched for anything that would stop it.
    2. 
  
  2. The threats of Lesson 1.3. For each attack type you know (password guessing, malware, data theft, denial of service, unauthorized change), ask what it would look like on this asset. This is the “what could go wrong” of the method below.
    3. 
  
  3. Policies and law. Québec's Law 25 obliges an organization that holds personal information to keep a register of its confidentiality incidents (unauthorized access, use, communication or loss of personal information) and, when an incident presents a risk of serious injury, to notify the Commission d'accès à l'information du Québec and the persons concerned. An organization that cannot detect an incident cannot record it or notify anyone: the law itself creates the need “detect unauthorized access to files containing personal information”.
    4. 
  
  4. Operational objectives. The course description says that preventative monitoring detects problems before they cause damage. A backup that silently fails, a disk that fills up, an Internet link that drops are not attacks, but they are exactly what the owner expects monitoring to notice first.
    5. 
  
  5. Past incidents and the owner's concerns. Val-des-Pins paid an invoice to a fraudulent account last year after a forged e-mail. That story becomes two needs on the cloud e-mail service: a sign-in from an unusual location, and a mailbox forwarding rule created without a request.
    6. 
  
  6. Reference lists published by recognized bodies. They save you from forgetting the ordinary needs. The Baseline Cyber Security Controls for Small and Medium Organizations of the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security (CCCS, part of the Communications Security Establishment) are written for organizations the size of Val-des-Pins. The CIS Controls of the Center for Internet Security (CIS) devote Control 8 to audit log management and Control 13 to network monitoring and defence. The Cybersecurity Framework of the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST CSF 2.0) has a Detect function whose category “Continuous Monitoring” lists what an organization is expected to watch. The MITRE ATT&CK (Adversarial Tactics, Techniques and Common Knowledge) knowledge base names, for each known attacker technique, the data sources in which it can be seen; you will use it in later courses of the program.

2.4.5 Coverage and gaps Slide 41


Once every asset has been through the six questions, two numbers summarize the result. Coverage is the share of needs for which at least one usable data source exists. A gap is a need with no usable data source. The coverage matrix shows both at a glance: one row per asset, one column per data source, and in each cell the number of needs that source covers on that asset. An asset whose row is empty, or a need whose sources are all blocked by a constraint, is visible immediately.


Every gap is recorded with three things: the constraint that causes it (its ID from the register of 2.3), a compensating measure if one exists (CNC-PC-01 cannot report its own logons, but the firewall sees every connection it makes), and the owner's decision if none exists (the printer keeps scanned documents but produces no log; the owner accepts the gap for now). Gaps feed Lesson 2.5 tomorrow (they weigh in the prioritization) and Lesson 6.3 later (Improving monitoring coverage).

Step-by-step explanation of Program 2


    
  
  1. ASSETS. Each asset is a dictionary: its identifier, its type (which decides which events can hit it), its three ratings c, i and a, and the list of data sources that the constraints of Lesson 2.3 leave available on it. CNC-PC-01 has only the firewall log and the availability check, because constraint C-04 forbids any software on it.
    2. 
  
  2. THREATS. Each event is a dictionary: the requirement(s) it harms, the asset types it can hit, the observable (with its number or pattern), the usable data sources in order of preference, and the detection time objective in minutes.
    3. 
  
  3. criticality. For one asset and one event, the function reads the asset's rating for each requirement the event harms and keeps the highest. Password guessing harms C and I; on SRV-FILE-01 both are 3, so the criticality is 3.
    4. 
  
  4. usable_source. The function walks through the event's preferred sources and returns the first one that the asset actually offers. When none matches it returns None, which the program records as a gap.
    5. 
  
  5. The double loop. Question 1 takes the assets one by one; question 2 takes every event and skips those that cannot hit this asset type (a printer has no logon to guess). For the applicable ones, questions 3 to 5 are read from the event, question 4 is answered by usable_source, and the need is assembled with an identifier N-01, N-02, …
    6. 
  
  6. COVERAGE. A dictionary counts, for each pair (asset, source), how many needs that source covers on that asset. It is the coverage matrix.
    7. 
  
  7. The summary. After the loops the program counts the needs, the covered ones and the gaps, prints every gap with the reason a plan must document, and prints the matrix with a dot where a source covers nothing.
    8. 
  
  8. The CSV file. The register is written to monitoring_needs_register.csv with one column per answer of the method: asset, requirement, event, observable, data source, detection objective, criticality and whether it is covered. It is the spreadsheet you attach to your Lab 1 plan.
    9. 
  
  9. Reading the result. 27 needs; 24 covered; 3 gaps, all on CNC-PC-01 and all caused by the same constraint. The gaps are not failures of the method: they are the method doing its job, showing where the constraints of 2.3 limit what 2.4 can promise.
    10. 
  
  10. What is not in this program. Prioritization. The criticality column is an input for Lesson 2.5 tomorrow, where the needs are ordered according to the network's resources and its security requirements before the plan is finished.
