2.5 Prioritizing monitoring activities according to resources and security requirements

Class 7 turns the monitoring needs of Lesson 2.4 and the constraints of Lesson 2.3 into one ordered, defensible list of activities — the core of the monitoring plan you write in Lab 1 tonight.

Friday, September 25, 2026 · 6–10 pm · Online Reading time: 60 minutes Then: Lab 1 (5 %) — Integrative Project, Part 1 Competency EZ01 · element 2 · criterion 2.3

Before we begin 0:00–0:05

Topic 2 has one purpose: to understand the network well enough to decide what must be watched. Lesson 2.1 named the components, 2.2 taught you to read the documentation and the topology diagram, 2.3 listed the organizational and operational constraints (rules and limits that the monitoring must respect), and 2.4 identified the monitoring needs (what must be detected, on which assets). Tonight's lesson, 2.5, closes the topic by ordering those needs. The order is then written into a document called the monitoring plan, which is exactly what Lab 1 asks you to produce.

The lesson follows the wording of the competency. Element 2 of EZ01 (the competency code for "Monitor a computer network from a cybersecurity perspective") is "Analyze the topology of the network", and its third performance criterion reads: "Prioritization of surveillance activities in accordance with network resources and security requirements." Two inputs are named there — resources and security requirements — and the whole hour is organized around them.

What you will be able to do at the end of the hour

  1. Explain, with numbers, why a small organization cannot monitor everything and must choose.
  2. Rate the criticality of an asset from the impact of losing its confidentiality, integrity or availability.
  3. Rate the exposure of an asset from five facts that can be observed without touching the network.
  4. Compute a priority score, assign a monitoring tier, and fit the activities into a resource envelope.
  5. Record the result — including what is deferred and why — in the monitoring plan for Lab 1.

2.5.1

Why prioritize: two inputs, one order 0:05–0:18

Concepts: prioritization · monitoring activity · resources · security requirements · resource envelope · retention

Prioritization is the act of putting activities in order of importance, so that the most important ones are done first and receive the largest share of the available resources. In preventative monitoring, the activities being ordered are monitoring activities. A monitoring activity is a defined, repeatable observation of one part of the network — for example, "collect the connection log of FW-01 every day and review it every morning", or "capture the traffic of the switch port that serves CNC-PC-01". Each activity names a source of data, a way to collect it, and a way to review it.

Why is an order needed at all? Because no monitoring activity is free. Every one of them consumes resources, and a small organization has very few:

  • People and time. Someone must read what was collected. An alert that nobody reviews protects nothing. At Val-des-Pins, one technician has five hours per week for security work, and that time is shared with everything else in this course.
  • Storage. Logs and captures are files. A firewall can write several hundred MB (megabytes) of log lines per day; a full packet capture of an office network can write several GB (gigabytes) per day. Storage is finite, and the retention period — the length of time the collected data is kept — multiplies the need: 90 days of retention means 90 days of files on disk at the same time.
  • Processing capacity and bandwidth. A monitoring agent (a small program installed on a machine to report what happens on it) uses CPU (Central Processing Unit) time and RAM (Random Access Memory) on the machine it watches. Sending logs across the network uses bandwidth. A mirrored port (a switch port configured to copy the traffic of another port for observation) can drop packets when the copied traffic exceeds its capacity. Lesson 2.3 already told us where an agent is not permitted at all: CNC-PC-01, under the vendor contract.
  • Money and licences. Commercial monitoring tools are usually priced per monitored device or per GB (gigabyte) of data ingested per day. A free tool still costs the hours needed to learn and maintain it.

The second input is the set of security requirements: what the organization must protect, and how well. Requirements come from four places. First, laws: in Québec, Law 25 (the Act to modernize legislative provisions as regards the protection of personal information, which amended the Act respecting the protection of personal information in the private sector) obliges an enterprise to protect the personal information it holds — the payroll data on PC-ACCT-01, for instance. Second, contracts: the vendor agreement for the CNC machine, or the terms of the cloud e-mail provider. Third, internal policies: the owner's decision that customer drawings are confidential and that the guest Wi-Fi network is out of scope. Fourth, the monitoring needs you identified in Lesson 2.4: what must be detected, on which assets, and how quickly.

Place the two inputs side by side and a conflict appears immediately. The requirements describe what the organization would like to watch; the resources describe what it can afford to watch. Prioritization resolves the conflict in a defensible, written way, by answering three questions in a fixed order:

  1. Which assets matter most? — the criticality question (Lesson 2.5.2).
  2. Which assets are most likely to be attacked or to fail? — the exposure question (Lesson 2.5.3).
  3. Within the budget, which activities are done first, which are done in a reduced form, and which are deferred? — the fitting question (Lesson 2.5.4).

Two ideas frame the whole lesson. The first is that prioritization is risk-based: an asset's place in the order depends on the harm its loss would cause and on the likelihood of that loss. These are the two ingredients of a risk assessment — a structured evaluation of the risks an organization faces — as described in Special Publication 800-30 of NIST (the National Institute of Standards and Technology, the United States standards body whose free publications are used worldwide). The second idea is that prioritization is explicit: a decision that is not written down cannot be reviewed, defended or improved. The product of tonight's work is a written list, and that list is the heart of the monitoring plan.

One term needs a precise definition now, because everything later must fit inside it. The resource envelope is the total amount of each resource that the organization has decided to dedicate to monitoring, expressed in units that can be measured: MB (megabytes) per day of storage, minutes per day of analyst time, and the number of tool licences. The envelope is agreed with the owner and written in the plan; it is not the technician's private estimate. Canadian guidance for small organizations reasons in exactly this way: the CCCS (Canadian Centre for Cyber Security) publishes baseline controls for small and medium organizations that openly aim to obtain most of the benefit from a fraction of the effort — which is only possible when the effort is directed to the right place first.

Common beginner error

"We will collect everything now and sort it out later." Collected data that nobody reviews is worse than useless. It costs storage; it creates a legal duty, because logs and captures contain personal information and must be protected (Legal Notice, sections 6.7 and 7.2); and it hides the few important events among millions of unimportant ones — the "quality of results" problem you will meet again in Topic 6.

Criticality: rating what an asset is worth to the organization 0:18–0:31

      
Concepts: asset · criticality · impact · high-water mark · data classification · RTO · single point of failure · exclusion

    

    
  

  

  

    
An asset is anything of value to the organization that the network contains or supports: a device, a service, or a set of data. Lesson 2.1 and your Class 6 inventory listed the assets of Val-des-Pins; tonight each of them receives a criticality rating. Criticality is the measure of how much the organization would suffer if the asset stopped doing its job, or if the data it holds were disclosed or corrupted. It is a statement about consequences, not about attackers: the criticality of the file server does not change when a new vulnerability is published; it changes when the business changes — for example, when the workshop starts storing customer credit-card numbers.

    
The rating method used in this course is borrowed from a public standard, Publication 199 of the FIPS (Federal Information Processing Standards) series issued by NIST (the National Institute of Standards and Technology), because it is simple and because it maps directly onto the CIA (Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability) triad from Topic 1. For each asset you ask three questions, and you answer each with a number from 1 to 3:

    
    
      
  • Confidentiality impact. If the data stored on, or passing through, this asset were read by someone who should not read it, what would happen? 1 — limited: public or harmless information. 2 — serious: internal information; embarrassment; some cost. 3 — severe: personal information protected by law, trade secrets, passwords, financial data.
    • 
      
  • Integrity impact. If the data or the configuration were modified without authorization, what would happen? 1 — limited: easy to notice and to correct. 2 — serious: wrong decisions or wrong products for a while. 3 — severe: a safety risk, a financial loss, corrupted backups, physical damage.
    • 
      
  • Availability impact. If the asset stopped working, what would happen, and how fast would it matter? 1 — limited: an inconvenience; a workaround exists. 2 — serious: a team stops for hours. 3 — severe: the whole organization stops, or a legal or contractual deadline is missed.
    • 
    

    
The asset's criticality is the highest of the three numbers. This is called the high-water mark rule: one severe impact is enough to make the asset critical, whatever the two other answers are. The backup unit is the clearest illustration. Nobody works on NAS-01 during the day, so its availability impact is only 2; but a corrupted backup is discovered on the day it is needed, when nothing else is left, so its integrity impact is 3 — and NAS-01 is critical.

    
Two helpers make the availability answer easier. The RTO (Recovery Time Objective) is the longest interruption the organization can tolerate for a given asset before the damage becomes serious; a short RTO means a high availability impact. A single point of failure is a component whose failure stops everything that depends on it: FW-01 is a single point of failure for Internet access and for the hosted e-mail, and SW-01 is a single point of failure for the whole server room.

    
One helper makes the confidentiality answer easier: data classification, the practice of labelling data by its sensitivity — public, internal, confidential, personal information. At Val-des-Pins, personal information is the most sensitive label, because Law 25 imposes obligations and administrative penalties on the enterprise that holds it, and the CAI (Commission d'accès à l'information du Québec, the provincial body that oversees this law) can investigate a complaint. Any asset that stores or processes personal information receives a confidentiality impact of 3 without further debate.

    
Three rules keep the ratings consistent:

    
    
      
  1. Rate the impact on the organization, never the replacement price of the device. A 400 $ firewall can be the most critical asset in the building.
    2. 
      
  2. Rate assets that hold the same data and play the same role as a group — the five office workstations, for instance. It saves time and produces consistent ratings. Note in the register that they were rated as a group.
    3. 
      
  3. Write one sentence of justification per asset. A number without a reason cannot be defended when the owner asks "why is the printer a 1 and the switch a 3?".
    4. 
    

    
Finally, an asset that the owner excluded from monitoring in Lesson 2.3 — the guest Wi-Fi network — is not rated. It is recorded as an exclusion, with the reason and the date of the decision. Exclusions belong in the plan: an auditor, or the technician who replaces you next year, must be able to see that the gap was a decision and not an oversight.

    2.5.3
    

      
Exposure: rating how likely trouble is 0:31–0:44

      
Concepts: threat · vulnerability · exposure · likelihood · five exposure factors · CVE, NVD, CVSS · review date

    

    
  

  

  

    
Criticality answers "how bad would it be?". Exposure answers "how likely is it?". The exposure of an asset is the degree to which it can be reached by a threat — any potential cause of an unwanted incident: an attacker, a malicious program, a human error, a hardware failure — combined with the degree to which that threat could succeed because of a vulnerability, a weakness in the asset or in the way it is used. In formal risk assessment this is the likelihood side of the risk equation: Special Publication 800-30 of NIST (the National Institute of Standards and Technology) describes risk as a function of the likelihood of an event and of its impact. Criticality gave us the impact; exposure gives us the likelihood.

    
A beginner cannot compute a probability for a small workshop; there is no history to compute it from. What a beginner can do is observe five facts about each asset, because they are visible in the documentation, in the device's own configuration screens, or in the interviews done in Lesson 2.3. Each fact is scored 0 when absent and 1 when present:

    
    
      
  • E1 — Internet contact. The asset is reachable from the Internet (an open port, a port-forwarding rule, a remote-management service), or it regularly receives content from the Internet (e-mail attachments, downloads, web browsing).
    • 
      
  • E2 — Known weakness. The asset runs an unsupported operating system or firmware, is missing security updates, or still uses a default or shared password.
    • 
      
  • E3 — People and media. The asset is used by many people, through a shared account, from a location where anyone can plug in a cable, or with removable media such as USB (Universal Serial Bus) sticks in daily use.
    • 
      
  • E4 — Privilege. The asset holds or uses administrative credentials, keys or access rights that would let an attacker reach other assets from it. Security professionals call the set of assets reachable from a compromised one its blast radius; the term is a plain description of what would be affected, not a figure of speech.
    • 
      
  • E5 — History. An incident, a malware detection or suspicious activity involving the asset was recorded in the last 12 months.
    • 
    

    
The exposure score is the sum of the five factors, from 0 to 5, and it is converted to a rating on the same 1–3 scale as criticality: a score of 0 or 1 is Low (1); 2 or 3 is Moderate (2); 4 or 5 is High (3). Using the same scale on both axes is what allows the two ratings to be combined in the next lesson.

    
Why these five factors and not others? Because they correspond to the ways small organizations are most often compromised, as reported year after year in the threat assessments of the CCCS (Canadian Centre for Cyber Security): malicious e-mail and phishing (E1); unpatched or end-of-life systems (E2); weak or shared credentials and removable media (E3); abuse of administrative accounts (E4). The fifth factor is there because a past incident is the best available predictor of a future one (E5).

    
Two properties of exposure matter for the rest of the course. First, exposure is independent of criticality. PC-SHOP-01, the shared computer on the shop floor, holds nothing of value, but it scores 4 on exposure; NAS-01 is critical but scores 1. Both numbers are needed, and Lesson 2.5.4 combines them. Second, exposure changes: a firmware update removes E2, a past incident ages out of E5 after twelve months, and a new remote-access rule adds E1 overnight. Every exposure rating therefore carries a review date, and the register is re-read whenever the network changes.

    
Public information helps with factor E2. The CVE (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures) list is the public catalogue of known vulnerabilities, each with a unique identifier; the NVD (National Vulnerability Database, operated by NIST) publishes the same entries with a CVSS (Common Vulnerability Scoring System) severity score from 0 to 10. A device running a version with a known critical vulnerability receives E2 = 1 without discussion. Reading these lists is observation. Scanning a network to discover versions is an intrusive act, reserved for the course 420-3C1-DW Managing Vulnerabilities and for designated targets only: in this course you take versions from documentation, from the device's own screen, or from the owner (Legal Notice, sections 6.3 and 7.6).

    

      
Legal reminder — facts about people

      
Factors E3 and E5 involve information about employees ("a phishing message was opened on PC-ACCT-01 in June"). Keep it factual, minimal and about the asset; never name the person in the register, and never use the register to observe or profile an individual. Monitoring a person — a colleague, a classmate, a relative — is never a course activity and may be a criminal offence (Legal Notice, sections 6.3 and 7.2).

    2.5.4
    

      
From two ratings to an order — and to the monitoring plan 0:44–0:55

      
Concepts: priority score · priority matrix · monitoring tier · tie-breaking · reduced activity · deferred activity · override · monitoring plan · review cycle

    

    
  

  

  

    
The priority score of an asset is its criticality multiplied by its exposure, a number from 1 to 9. The two ratings are multiplied rather than added because an asset needs both a serious consequence and a real likelihood to deserve continuous attention. A critical asset that nothing can reach (3 × 1 = 3) and a wide-open asset that holds nothing (1 × 3 = 3) both land in the middle; an asset that is critical and exposed (3 × 3 = 9) lands on top. With addition, the first two would score 4 and the third 6 — the difference between "worth watching" and "watch first" would almost disappear.

    
The priority matrix displays all assets at once: a grid of three rows (criticality) by three columns (exposure), each cell coloured by the score it contains. Risk management uses the same drawing under the names risk matrix or heat map; the hero image at the top of this page is the matrix of Val-des-Pins. Cells with a score of 6 to 9 are High, 3 to 4 are Moderate, 1 to 2 are Low.

    
The score decides the monitoring tier — the level of effort assigned to an asset. Three tiers are enough for a small network:

  

  
Table 4 — Monitoring tiers used in this course

  

    

      
TierScoreCollectionAlertsReview by the technicianRetention

      
        
Tier 16–9Continuous (agent or syslog), started first in Lab 2Automatic, immediate, on the predefined suspicious behaviours (Lesson 3.5)Every working day90 days

        
Tier 23–4Continuous or a periodic exportFew; a weekly summaryWeekly30 days

        
Tier 31–2No dedicated collectionNoneIncluded in the periodic baseline check (Lesson 3.4); re-rated when something changes—

      
    

  

  

    
The tier says how an asset is watched; the order of work says when. To obtain the order, sort the assets by score, highest first, and settle ties with three tie-breaking rules, applied in sequence: the higher criticality comes first; then the higher exposure; then the activity with the lower daily cost, because a cheaper activity gives protection sooner for the same budget.

    
Then the list is fitted into the resource envelope of Lesson 2.5.1. Walk down the sorted list and add each activity to the plan as long as both running totals — storage and analyst time — stay inside the envelope. When a Tier 1 activity does not fit, do not drop it: try a reduced activity first — filtered logs instead of all logs, connection records (which addresses spoke to which, and when) instead of a full packet capture, or sampling. Reduced activities are the everyday work of Lesson 6.2, reducing the impact of monitoring on the network. Everything that still does not fit becomes a deferred activity: an accepted gap, written in the plan with its reason and a review date. A gap that is written down is a decision; a gap that is not written down is a surprise.

    
The computed order is a proposal, not a verdict. The technician reads it and may change it with an override — a documented change of the computed order, with a reason. Two reasons are accepted without argument: a dependency (the asset is the recovery path for other assets, as NAS-01 is for every Tier 1 asset) and a legal obligation (an asset holding personal information cannot be deferred without the owner's written decision). Any other override must be explained in the plan and signed off by the owner.

    
All of this is recorded in the monitoring plan, the document that Lab 1 asks you to produce. A plan is not a list of tools; it is the written reasoning that turns an inventory into an ordered set of activities. Its sections follow the four lessons of Topic 2 and the two inputs of tonight's criterion:

The plan is never finished. Three events trigger a review of the priorities: a change in the network (a new asset, a new Internet-facing service, a new remote-access rule), an incident involving any asset (which sets E5 to 1 and often reveals a wrong rating), and the fixed schedule — quarterly is reasonable for a business of this size. Each review is the moment when the reference database is updated (Lesson 6.1) and when deferred activities are reconsidered (Lesson 6.3, improving coverage). This is why every rating in the register carries a review date.

Step by step — what the simulator does

    6.
